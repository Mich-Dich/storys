



The hum of the fluorescent lights was the loudest sound in the entire building.
I'd noticed it before, of course. Day after day, year after year, the same faint buzz threading through the silence like a mosquito that never got close enough to swat. Tonight it seemed louder. Or maybe the building was just emptier.
I blinked, and the spreadsheet in front of me blurred into meaningless white cells. Q3 revenue projections. I'd been staring at the same column for forty minutes. Not stuck. Not confused. Just... not moving. Like a cursor waiting for input.
The clock on the wall ticked to 9:17 PM.
Day 4,127. That's what I called it, privately. Four thousand, one hundred and twenty-seven days since I'd started this job. Give or take. The number wasn't exact, but the feeling was. Day 4,127 felt identical to Day 1,000. Identical to Day 2,500. The same chair. The same monitor. The same quiet, polite, forgettable John Doe, staying late because there was nothing waiting for him at home except more silence.
Identical. Just older.
I saved the spreadsheet out of muscle memory and stood up, stretching. My back cracked. The window behind my desk looked out over the city skyline — towers of glass and steel, their lights blinking in the darkness like a circuit board. Pretty, in a sterile way. I'd always thought so.
But something was different tonight.
A fog was rolling in between the buildings. Thick. Reddish. It caught the streetlights and glowed faintly, like diluted blood backlit by neon. I watched it curl around the base of the tower across the street, swallowing the lobby. It moved strangely — too fast, too deliberate. Like it had somewhere to be.
*Weather phenomenon*, I thought. *Or a fire somewhere. Maybe a chemical spill.*
I yawned.
Not because I was tired. I was never really tired. I was never really anything. Just... hollow. A shell walking through a script, waiting for a cue that never came.
I turned away from the window, grabbed my jacket from the back of my chair, and slung it over my arm. Might as well go home. Sleep. Wake up. Day 4,128.
That was when I heard the first thump.
It was wet. That was the first thing I noticed.
A wet, heavy sound against the glass door at the end of the office floor. Like someone had thrown a soaked gym bag against it. I paused, one arm in my jacket sleeve, and listened.
Thump.
Another one.
I should have felt something then, shouldn't I? Fear, maybe. Unease. The primal alarm bell that tells a person *something is wrong*. But all I felt was a mild curiosity. Like finding an unexpected email in your inbox. Huh. What's this?
I walked toward the glass door.
The hallway beyond was dim, lit only by the emergency exit signs casting red pools on the carpet. And pressed against the glass, her cheek smearing a dark streak across the surface, was Susan from accounting.
I recognized her immediately. She worked on the floor below mine. Mid-forties, always wore cardigans with floral patterns, brought homemade cookies for the quarterly meetings. We'd spoken maybe three times. She'd asked me once if I preferred chocolate chip or oatmeal raisin, and I'd said chocolate chip, and that had been the entirety of our relationship.
She didn't look like Susan anymore.
Her eyes were wrong. Milky. The pupils clouded over like a dog with cataracts, but somehow still tracking — twitching, locking onto me through the glass. Black veins spider-webbed across her cheeks and down her neck, pulsing faintly. Her mouth hung open, and a dark fluid that wasn't quite blood dripped from her chin, smearing on the glass as she pressed forward.
She growled.
Not a human growl. Not a sound a person makes. Low, guttural, vibrating from somewhere deep in her chest. The glass hummed with it.
I stood there, four feet away, and watched.
My pulse quickened. I felt it in my throat, a flutter of something. Not fear. I'd been scared before — watching horror movies as a kid, almost getting hit by a car once. This wasn't that. This was... interest. Curiosity sharpening into something else. Something I didn't have a name for.
I stepped closer.
"Susan?" I said. My voice came out steady. Calm. "Are you okay?"
She slammed her palm against the glass. Hard. The door rattled in its frame.
I should have run. I should have called 911. I should have done any of the things a normal person would do in that moment. But I didn't. Instead, I reached for the door handle. Just a crack, I told myself. Just to see what she would do.
I pulled the door open maybe four inches.
She lunged.
Her arm shot through the gap, fingers clawing toward my face, and I reacted on pure instinct — I slammed the door shut. The edge caught her forearm and there was a wet *crack*, bone snapping under the force. Her arm bent at an angle that arms shouldn't bend.
She didn't scream.
She didn't flinch.
She just kept clawing, the broken arm scraping against the door frame, dark blood smearing the metal. Her milky eyes stayed locked on me, and that low growl never stopped.
I stared at her broken arm. At the complete absence of pain in her face.
*Interesting.*
The word surfaced in my mind, calm and clear, like a note on a scientific observation. She didn't feel it. Normal people felt pain. Normal people recoiled. Susan wasn't a normal person anymore. She was something else entirely. Something that didn't think, didn't fear, didn't stop.
The door buckled. She was pushing through, broken arm and all, her weight slamming against the frame. I stepped back, and she tumbled into the office, catching herself on a cubicle wall, already lurching toward me.
I grabbed the first thing my hand found on the nearest desk.
A metal water bottle. Stainless steel. Heavy.
I swung it at her head.
The impact jarred up my arm, a satisfying *clang* that echoed through the empty office floor. Susan's head snapped to the side. She stumbled, one knee buckling — and then straightened right back up, her milky eyes refocusing on me like nothing had happened.
The dent in her temple didn't seem to bother her.
I swung again. And again. Each hit knocked her back a step, but she kept coming, arms outstretched, fingers grasping. The growl grew louder, more frantic. I was hitting her hard enough to fracture a skull, and she just. Kept. Coming.
Some distant part of my brain — the rational part, the part trained by decades of society — was screaming that this was wrong. That this was a person. Susan. Cardigans. Chocolate chip cookies.
But that voice was very small, and very far away, and growing quieter by the second.
I was breathing hard now. My shirt was soaked with sweat, my arms burning from the effort. The water bottle slipped from my grip and clattered to the floor. Susan lunged, and I barely sidestepped, her nails raking across my shoulder, tearing fabric but not skin.
She didn't think. That was the problem. She didn't react to pain, didn't hesitate, didn't calculate. I'd been fighting her like a person, trying to hurt her enough to make her stop. But whatever Susan was now, pain didn't register.
I had to *break* something.
She charged again, arms wide, mouth gaping, and this time I didn't dodge. I ducked low, caught her around the waist, and drove forward with my legs. We crashed into the metal filing cabinet against the wall. The corner of it — sharp, solid steel — caught her skull.
She kept clawing at my back.
I pulled her back and slammed her head into the corner again. Harder. A wet crunch. Still clawing. Again. The sound changed — softer, wetter. Again. Her movements slowed. Again. Her arms dropped to her sides.
Again.
She stopped moving.
I let go and she crumpled to the floor, a heap of floral cardigan and broken bones and dark blood pooling beneath her. The fluorescent lights hummed overhead, oblivious.
I stood there, breathing.
Just breathing.
My chest heaved. Sweat dripped from my chin, mixing with the dark spatter on my shirt. My arms trembled. My legs felt like rubber. I'd never been so exhausted in my entire life. Every muscle screamed. My knuckles were split. My shoulder burned where she'd clawed me.
And I was laughing.
It started small — a chuckle, barely audible, just a puff of air through my nose. Then it grew. A laugh. A real, genuine, full-chested laugh that echoed through the empty office and bounced off the cubicle walls. I slid down the filing cabinet and sat on the floor, back against the cold metal, and I laughed until my stomach hurt.
I couldn't remember the last time I'd laughed like that. Really laughed. Maybe childhood? Maybe never. But this — this was something else entirely. Every nerve in my body was alive. Every sense sharpened to a razor's edge. The gray fog that had followed me for as long as I could remember had lifted, just for a moment, and underneath it was something electric.
*That was fun.*
The thought surfaced unbidden, and I didn't push it away. I let it sit there, warm and bright, a little sun in my chest.
That was the most fun I'd had in my entire life.
I looked at Susan's body. At the dark pool spreading across the carpet. At the dent in the filing cabinet. At my trembling, bloodied hands.
I should have felt horror. Guilt. Nausea. Something.
But I felt... light. Like waking up from a nap you didn't know you were taking. Like the first breath after swimming underwater for too long. The hollowness was still there, somewhere beneath the surface, but for the first time in four thousand days, it was quiet.
I wiped my hands on my pants and stood up.
My eyes drifted to the office supplies scattered around me. The stapler on Susan's desk. The box of pens. The heavy-duty tape dispenser. The broom leaning against the wall by the supply closet.
I looked at them for a long moment.
Then I picked up the broom.
And I smiled.










I picked up the broom.
It was a cheap thing — plastic handle, synthetic bristles, the kind that bends if you put any real weight on it. But I wasn’t seeing a broom anymore. I was seeing potential. The handle, separated from the head, would be a staff. A good staff. Light, but solid enough. The bristles could be burned off. The end could be sharpened.
I turned it over in my hands, already imagining the weight of it after modification, and I smiled.
Then the world outside exploded.
The sound hit first — a deep, gut-rattling *crump* that shook the entire building and rattled the glass in its frame. I felt it in my chest, in my teeth. A second later, the office windows facing the street blazed orange-white, a sudden sun blooming in the darkness beyond the glass. The light was so intense it seared away every shadow, painting the cubicles in stark black and white, and I threw an arm up instinctively to shield my eyes.
When the glare faded, I was already moving toward the window.
I pressed my palms against the cool glass and looked down.
Two blocks away, a skyscraper was wearing a crown of fire. The helicopter — I could still make out the mangled tail rotor jutting from the inferno — had punched straight into the building's upper floors. Debris rained into the street, glittering shards of glass and twisted metal. The fireball was already collapsing into a churning column of black smoke, but the building itself was catching, flames licking from a dozen shattered windows.
And below, the city was screaming.
It wasn't one scream. It was a chorus. Dozens of voices, hundreds, threading together into a single raw note of chaos. Car alarms howled. Distant gunshots popped like firecrackers. And through the red mist rolling through the streets, I could see movement — figures, some running, some shambling, some falling under the weight of others.
I could see the zombies.
They were everywhere. A pack of them swarming an overturned bus. Another group clustered around something writhing on the sidewalk. I watched a man in a suit sprint across an intersection, tie flapping, only to be blindsided by two shapes that lurched out of an alley. They took him down with the mechanical efficiency of predators, no thought, just hunger. He didn't get back up.
The red fog was thicker now. It curled around streetlights like a living thing, filling the avenues, spilling into parks. It seemed to pulse with a faint inner light, and wherever it touched, things were dying or changing. I could see a dog — a German shepherd — convulsing on its side near a fire hydrant. Its legs jerked. Then it went still. Then it rose again, movements wrong, head twitching in that same mindless way Susan had.
I watched it all without blinking.
This wasn't a building lockdown. This wasn't an isolated incident. The crashed helicopter, the screams stretching to the horizon, the fires blooming across the skyline like a rash — this was the whole city. Maybe further. Maybe everywhere.
I should have been terrified. Any normal person would have been paralyzed, weeping, praying. But I just stood there, forehead against the glass, and felt a slow, warm certainty settle in my chest.
The world had ended.
And for the first time in my life, I was exactly where I belonged.
My breath fogged the glass as I exhaled. My reflection stared back at me, blood-smeared and grinning, eyes bright with something that had been buried for decades. The hollow man from the spreadsheet was gone. In his place was someone who had just woken up to a world full of purpose.
I turned away from the window and walked back to the office supplies.
Time to get to work.

**Scene 4: The Awakening**
The broom came apart with a satisfying crack. I wedged the handle under my foot and yanked upward, snapping the plastic head clean off. The handle itself was hollow aluminum — light, quick, exactly what I wanted. I ran my thumb along the break point and found it jagged enough to do some damage, but I could do better.
The pencil sharpener was mounted on the wall near the supply closet. Old-school, heavy steel, the kind that chewed through cedar like butter. I cranked the handle experimentally. Still sharp. I fed the broom handle into it and started turning.
The sound it made was deeply satisfying. A steady rasp, each rotation carving the aluminum into a tapered point. It took a few minutes. I didn't hurry. I hummed while I worked — some tune I couldn't name, maybe something from childhood, maybe something I made up on the spot. The moans from the hallway grew louder, closer, but they were just background noise. A soundtrack.
The point came out wickedly sharp. I tested it against a stack of printer paper and it punched through all five hundred sheets like a javelin. Good. But I wanted it harder. I flicked my lighter and ran the flame along the tip, heating the metal until it glowed faintly, then let it cool. The oxidation darkened the aluminum. Harder now. Better.
Next, the stapler.
It was one of those heavy-duty Swingline models, the kind that could punch through a hundred pages without jamming. I picked it up. Too blunt on its own, but the three-hole punch beside it was a solid chunk of steel. I tore strips of duct tape from a roll I found in the supply closet and lashed them together — stapler on one end, hole punch on the other — with a length of computer cable as a chain. A flail. Crude, unbalanced, but when I swung it experimentally against a filing cabinet, it left a dent the size of my fist. It would break bones. It would break skulls.
I kept going.
Letter opener taped to the telescopic presentation pointer. It made a sort of short spear, collapsible, concealable. The pointer was designed to extend at the push of a button — I tested it and the letter opener shot out with a satisfying *thwip*. A surprise weapon. Something for close quarters.
A box cutter from the mailroom supplies became a push dagger, its blade fixed between my fingers with tape and fury.
By the time I finished, I was surrounded by an arsenal of improvised death. I'd arranged them on the floor around me like a child setting out toys on Christmas morning. The broom-spear was my favorite — light, elegant, brutal. I picked it up and turned it over in my hands, admiring the way the red-tinted light from outside glinted off the fire-hardened point.
The moans from the hallway were unmistakable now. Not one voice. Several. A chorus of the damned, harmonizing with the distant screams from the city below.
I stood up. The flail went over my shoulder. The spear in my right hand. The push dagger in my belt loop. The collapsible surprise spear in my back pocket, like a deadly pen.
I was ready.
I walked toward the hallway door, and my grin widened until it hurt my cheeks.

**Scene 5: The First Spree**
The hallway was dark and long, stretching the length of the office floor with cubicles on either side like a maze of cloth walls and cheap carpet. Emergency lights cast red pools at intervals, just enough to see shapes moving. Three of them, at the far end. They hadn't noticed me yet. They were shuffling aimlessly, bumping into walls, growling softly.
Three. I could handle three.
But why stop at three?
I crouched low and slid into the maze of cubicles, using the fabric walls as cover. My heart was pounding again, that same electric rhythm from before. Every nerve was singing. I could feel the sweat on my palms, the weight of the spear, the slight creak of the flail against my shoulder.
I reached the first zombie before it knew I was there.
It was a man from IT — I recognized the lanyard around his neck, the company logo swinging with each shuffle. His glasses were still on, one lens cracked, the milky eyes behind them staring at nothing. I came up behind him, silent, spear reversed in my grip. The point slid into the base of his skull with almost no resistance, a soft wet sound like stepping into mud.
He dropped.
I pulled the spear free and kept moving.
*Kill two.*
The next one was closer to the others, so I had to be careful. I took a roll of cables from a supply shelf and strung a tripwire at ankle height between two cubicle walls. Then I rapped the spear against a filing cabinet — *clang* — and watched them turn. Two of them, a man and a woman, both in business casual, both with those hungry growls. They lurched toward the sound, feet shuffling, arms reaching.
The woman hit the tripwire first. She went down hard, face-first into the carpet, and before she could push herself up, I was on her. The flail came off my shoulder and down in a single arc, the stapler end catching her skull with a crack that echoed through the floor. She stopped moving.
*Kill three.*
The man kept coming, oblivious to his fallen companion. I stepped back, leading him into a dead-end cubicle. He followed, arms outstretched, and when he was committed, I sidestepped and shoved a desk chair into his path. He stumbled. I drove the spear through his temple.
*Kill four.*
I was breathing hard now, but I wasn't tired. I was awake. More awake than I'd ever been. I wiped the spear on a discarded jacket and kept moving.
The floor was wide, a labyrinth of cubicles and conference rooms, and I hunted through it like something born for this. Each kill taught me something new. Bashing took more effort but was immediate — a skull caved in, the body dropped. Piercing was cleaner but risked getting the weapon stuck. Stealth kills, from behind, were the most efficient. But the noisy ones, the ones where I let them see me, where I let them almost reach me before I struck — those were the most fun.
I lured them with sound. I herded them with obstacles. I separated them like a wolf culling the weak from a herd. In a glass-walled conference room, I trapped two of them behind a table and picked them off one at a time, using the collapsible pointer to surprise the second when it turned toward the noise of its companion falling.
I was laughing again. Not loud, not crazy-sounding — just a low chuckle, a constant undercurrent of joy that I couldn't suppress. Every kill was a hit of pure, undiluted pleasure. The moment of impact, the sudden stillness, the knowledge that I had won and they had lost. It was better than coffee. Better than sex. Better than anything I'd ever felt in the gray blur of my old life.
By the time I reached the end of the hallway, I'd lost count. I had to stop and mentally tally the bodies in my wake. Susan, from before. The IT guy. The tripped woman. The man in the cubicle. Two in the conference room. A security guard near the elevators. Another man near the break room, still clutching a half-eaten granola bar. A woman in the restroom, surprised mid-crouch. A young guy — intern, maybe — near the printers.
Eleven.
No, wait. A twitching figure near the water cooler. I finished it with a stomp to the head. Twelve.
Twelve kills.
I leaned against the wall near the floor's main entrance, the red emergency light washing over me. I was drenched in sweat. My shirt was a Jackson Pollock of dark fluids. My arms ached. My knuckles were raw. The spear was bent slightly at the tip from one too many impacts.
And I was so, so happy.
I tilted my head back and closed my eyes, just breathing, just letting the joy wash over me. The moans from the stairwell were distant now. The screaming outside was a lullaby. I could have stayed there forever, basking in the afterglow of the hunt.
Then a cold chime echoed inside my skull.
My eyes snapped open.

**Scene 6: The Nexus Binds**
It wasn't a sound from the room. It was a sound *inside me*, like someone had struck a crystal bell directly against my brain. The resonance was ice-cold and sterile, utterly alien, and yet it felt... natural. Like a lock clicking open.
A panel appeared before my eyes.
I flinched — not in fear, but in surprise. The panel was translucent, blue-white, hovering in the air about two feet from my face. It didn't cast light on the walls. It didn't cast a shadow. It was just *there*, projected onto my vision like a heads-up display. I reached out to touch it and my fingers passed through, triggering a ripple of text.
The text scrolled into place, crisp and clean:
> **Nexus Initialization Protocol**  
> Congratulations, Participant. Your kill count has exceeded the threshold. You are now bound.  
> **Mechanic Unlocked: Blood Points (BP)**  
> Exchange kills for daily necessities, weapons, enhancements, and more.  
> Current BP: 12  
> **Welcome to the Game.**
I read it twice. Three times. The words were simple, but the implications unfolded in my mind like a flower blooming in time-lapse. Blood Points. Exchange kills. Daily necessities. Weapons. Enhancements. Game.
A store interface rotated into view, replacing the text. It was organized in neat categories — Food & Water, Weapons, Armor, Utilities, Skills, and a grayed-out tab labeled Advanced. I flicked through it with a thought, no hands needed. Bottled water: 1 BP. MREs: 2 BP. Combat knife: 5 BP. A crossbow: 15 BP. A "Locker Safe Zone" token: 10 BP, described as creating a twelve-hour sanctuary that repelled hostiles. Skills were more expensive, but there they were — Blade Proficiency, Silent Movement, Pain Resistance, Adrenaline Surge.
The system was clean. Elegant. Gamified.
It took the messy, exhausting reality of survival and turned it into a shopping list.
I stood there, bloody and bruised, staring at a floating menu in the middle of the apocalypse, and I understood with perfect clarity what had just happened. The universe — or whatever intelligence was behind this — had looked at me, John Doe, and said: *You want to kill? Here. Do it forever. We'll even pay you.*
Any normal person would have been horrified. They would have collapsed under the weight of the implications — the confirmation that this nightmare was permanent, global, and overseen by something unknowable. They would have wept at the thought of exchanging murder for currency, of being transformed into a cog in some cosmic slaughter machine.
Or they might have been relieved. A lifeline. A way to survive. Food, water, safety — all guaranteed, if they just played the game. A grim but necessary bargain.
I was neither horrified nor relieved.
I was overjoyed.
Because the first thought that crystallized in my mind, the thought that rose above all the others like a sun cresting the horizon, was this:
*I don't have to stop.*
I don't have to worry about food. I don't have to worry about sleep. I don't have to scavenge or ration or huddle in fear. The system will provide all of that. All I have to do — all I *want* to do — is keep going. Keep hunting. Keep killing. The things that have been chasing me through this office aren't just prey. They're currency. They're points in a game that never has to end.
The hollowness was gone. Really gone. In its place was a structure, a purpose, a reward loop tailored perfectly to the thing I'd been burying my entire life. I didn't have to pretend anymore. I didn't have to sit in a gray box and wait for day 4,128. The world was a hunting ground now, and I had just been issued a hunting license.
I laughed. Not the quiet chuckle from before. This was a deep, full laugh, the kind that shakes your shoulders and leaves you breathless. It echoed through the empty hallway, mingling with the distant moans and the crackle of fires beyond the windows.
I swiped through the store one more time, just to confirm it was real. Five BP for a combat knife. I selected it. A warm pulse ran through my palm, and a weight materialized in my hand — a black tactical knife, razor-sharp, perfectly balanced. It had simply *appeared*, as if it had always been there. I turned it over, watching the emergency lights glint off the blade, and my smile stretched so wide it almost hurt.
"Best overtime ever," I murmured.
I checked my BP: 7 remaining. I spent 1 on a bottle of water, just to test it. The bottle materialized in my other hand, cold and beaded with condensation. I cracked it open and drank, the water cutting through the coppery taste of blood in my mouth.
Then I turned back toward the stairwell. The moans were still there, but there was something else now — a notification chime, a quest marker floating at the edge of my vision:
> **New Objective: Descend.**  
> Clear the ground floor and exit the building.  
> Bonus: 20 BP for reaching the street before dawn.
I tightened my grip on the knife. The spear was spent, but I had a replacement now. I felt the familiar flutter of anticipation in my chest, the pre-hunt thrill. Somewhere below me, there were more kills waiting. More points. More fun.
The red-tinted world outside the windows was still burning, still screaming, still dying.
And I was exactly where I belonged.
