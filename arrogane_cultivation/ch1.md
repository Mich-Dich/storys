I was sitting in the place of honor at the head of the hall when the second set of memories arrived.

One moment, I was merely bored—watching the sect leader grovel with the detached interest one might give a trained monkey. The next, a torrent of images, sounds, and sensations flooded my mind. A world without qi. A life spent in front of glowing screens, a death so unremarkable it barely registered as a ripple in fate. I had been John Doe, a twenty-five-year-old office worker with no ambitions, no attachments, and a heart that pumped lukewarm blood through a life of muted greys.

And I had always been John Doe, the only son of the Doe family of the Upper Realm, a name that made even old monsters lower their eyes. The difference was that in this life, I had never known empathy. It simply hadn’t been necessary. In this world, strength was the only currency that mattered. A higher cultivation base, a superior technique, a drop of ancient bloodline—these were what separated humans from livestock. I had been born drowning in all three. The weak existed to be used, the strong existed to use them. That was the natural order.

The flood of memories sorted itself in seconds. The modern life was like watching a tedious play through dirty glass. It provided context, but it changed nothing. I was still the same man who looked down on these lower-realm insects from an infinite height.

I kept my eyes closed, letting my mind settle while the farce continued around me. The sect leader’s voice oozed through the incense-heavy air.

“…this humble one would be eternally grateful if Young Master Doe would accept my daughter, Lily, as a token of our sincerity. She is gentle, pure, and has never known a man’s touch. Merely a small gift to ease your stay in our humble realm.”

*A small gift.* The man was offering his own blood as if she were a particularly pleasing tea set. I didn’t open my eyes. The girl, Lily, was probably trembling like a plucked flower. Pretty, perhaps. Useful for pouring tea. That was the extent of her value. I would decide later whether to accept the gift or discard it. Livestock did not get a say.

Then a voice cut through the sycophantic murmuring, shrill and desperate.

“Sect Leader, you can’t! You’re sending Lily into a fire pit! She’s not a bargaining chip—she’s a human being!”

Ah. A bleating sheep. I didn’t bother to look. The voice was young, male, filled with the kind of righteous stupidity that only flourished in the lower realms. He had feelings for the girl, obviously. Love, or whatever these insects mistook for it. He thought he was saving her. In reality, he was a gnat buzzing near a dragon’s snout, too insignificant to even swat.

The sect leader’s spiritual pressure spiked, but before he could crush the fool, another voice rang out from nearer to my seat. One of the elder disciples, positioned on the steps below my dais, decided to earn some favor.

“Impudent trash! How dare you slander the Young Master in his presence!”

I felt the shift of qi, the gathering of a technique—a palm strike laced with spirit energy, meant to send the idiot crashing through the wall and possibly out of existence. The elder disciple was at the late Foundation Establishment stage. Respectable, for this backwater. The idiot, by all logic, should have been reduced to a bloodstain.

A thunderous crack split the air. Then gasps—shocked, disbelieving—rippled through the hall.

Slowly, I opened my eyes.

The idiot stood in the center of the hall, one arm raised. A barrier of shimmering azure light crackled around him, already fracturing but holding. His other hand was clenched at his side, knuckles white. His cultivation base—which must have been negligible moments ago—was now radiating at the early Core Formation stage. A sudden breakthrough, mid-attack, triggered by desperation and the need to protect his precious Lily. The elder disciple’s face was a mask of stunned humiliation. The sect leader had gone pale. Everyone in the hall, from the lowest servant to the highest elder, was staring at the boy as if he’d sprouted a second head.

I yawned.

The sound cut through the silence like a blade. Every head swiveled toward me. I let my gaze drift past the panting idiot as if he were a particularly uninteresting piece of furniture, and settled on the girl.

Lily. She was, in fact, quite pretty. Pale skin, hair like spilled ink, eyes wide with a terror that was swiftly morphing into a more complex emotion as she stared at her would-be savior. Her hands were clasped in front of her, knuckles white. She was worried about him. Of course she was. Probably the two of them had shared some fumbling, chaste little romance—glances across the training yard, maybe a whispered confession. They hadn’t even held hands, I suspected. The lower realm bred such tiresome innocence.

I lifted my teacup, still half-full with some mediocre spirit tea the sect had scraped together for my visit. The porcelain was decent. I held it out.

“Lily,” I said. My voice was calm, unhurried. “Pour me more tea.”

She flinched as if I’d struck her. Her eyes darted from me to the idiot—who was still frozen in his defensive stance, now trembling from the strain—and back again. The entire hall held its breath. The sect leader looked like a man watching his carefully stacked tower of jade topple in slow motion.

Lily’s feet moved before her mind caught up. She was well-trained, at least. She approached my table, picked up the teapot with shaking hands, and refilled my cup. The liquid trembled with her heartbeat, but not a drop spilled.

I watched her, tasting the fear radiating off her skin. It was the fear of prey that understood exactly how thin the thread above it was. Good. She wasn’t completely stupid.

When the cup was full, she retreated half a step and stood frozen, eyes downcast. I didn’t drink. Instead, I tilted my head, letting a small, curious smile touch my lips.

“You’re nervous,” I observed. “Is it for yourself? Or for that idiot over there, who’s about to die the moment his little barrier collapses?”

Her breath caught. A tiny, choked sound. Behind her, the idiot’s barrier finally shattered with a crystalline ping, and he stumbled, going to one knee. The elder disciple, rage and embarrassment warring on his face, was already forming another technique.

I didn’t acknowledge any of it. My eyes stayed on Lily. The girl who was nothing but livestock, trembling before a god.

“Tell me,” I said, voice soft as silk over a blade. “Do you want to save him?”

Her head snapped up. Her eyes, wet and desperate, met mine. She didn’t dare speak. She didn’t dare beg. She just stared, a rabbit transfixed by a wolf.

I let the silence stretch, savoring the absurdity of it all. A boy who thought love could overturn the natural order. A girl who thought her choices mattered. And me—the only person in this hall who understood that they were all just pieces on a board, and I was the hand that could sweep them away.

“If you ask nicely,” I said, “I might allow it.”


















The words had barely left my lips when Lily's legs gave out. She didn't collapse dramatically—there was no swooning maiden here, just a girl whose every last scrap of defiance had been scraped away. She dropped to her knees on the cold jade floor, her silk robes pooling around her like spilled ink, and bowed her forehead to the ground.

"Please," she whispered, voice cracking. "Young Master, please save him. I'll do anything. I'll be whatever you want. Just don't let him die."

Tears dripped onto the polished stone. Behind her, the idiot's barrier had fully shattered. He was on one knee, chest heaving, blood trickling from the corner of his mouth. The elder disciple's second attack was already mid-flight—a serpent of crimson flame coiling through the air, hungry and final. It would strike in less than two seconds. The sect leader stood frozen, too terrified of offending me to intervene. The other disciples and elders watched with bated breath, as cattle watch the slaughter of one of their own.

I wasn't looking at any of them.

Something had snagged at the edge of my perception. A flicker. An anomaly. I had been about to dismiss the whole affair as a mildly entertaining spectacle, but now… now a thought surfaced from the modern memories I had just digested. *If this were a story, that idiot would be the protagonist.* The hidden talent, the sudden breakthrough, the desperate defense of his love—it was all so painfully cliché. So I looked closer.

From my time in the Upper Realm, I had learned techniques that the insects of this lower world couldn't even dream of. One of them was Heaven's Gaze, a trivial little method that cost barely a wisp of qi. It let me perceive Fortune—the invisible thread of luck that the Heavens wove through every living creature. In the Upper Realm, it was a parlor trick, used by merchants sizing up competitors or by young masters deciding which servant might bring them less misfortune.

I activated it.

First, I saw myself.

Even knowing what to expect, the blaze of my own Fortune was staggering. A column of golden-white fire roared upward from my seated form, piercing the hall's ceiling, stretching toward the heavens themselves. The light of it filled my vision like a second sun. If any of these lower-realm insects possessed even a fraction of Heaven's Gaze, they would have been blinded the moment they looked at me. They would have fallen to their knees not out of fear, but out of sheer, overwhelming awe at the radiance of Heaven's favor burning in their midst. I was a bonfire in a world of dying embers.

I let the sight wash over me for a moment, then tuned it downward, dimming my perception until the hall around me came back into focus.

The sect leader: 3. A guttering candle. The elder disciples: 2, 4, 2, 5. The servants: 1, 1, 0. Some so utterly devoid of Heaven's favor that it was a miracle they drew breath at all. Grey. All of it grey.

Then my gaze slid to Lily, still prostrate at my feet. A soft glow, pale gold, clung to her slender frame. The number above her head read: 15.

I raised an eyebrow. Unusual, for this realm. A child born under a fortunate star, perhaps.

Then I looked at the idiot.

Above his kneeling, bleeding form, a number blazed: 20.

The golden shimmer clung to him like a second skin, pulsing faintly with each desperate beat of his heart. In a sea of threes and fours, it was a lighthouse in the fog. He was already pushing himself back to his feet, teeth gritted, eyes blazing with righteous fire. He hadn't given up. He was going to try to block the incoming attack with his bare body, even knowing it would kill him. Because he was that kind of fool.

A slow, genuine smile spread across my face. It was not a kind smile, but it wasn't predatory either. It was the smile of a man who had been dying of thirst and just spotted a puddle. Not pure water—barely drinkable—but something to wet the tongue.

A protagonist. An actual, Heaven-touched protagonist, right here in this backwater sect. And his luck was a 20. To anyone else in the Upper Realm, a 20 would be laughable. A fortune that tiny wouldn't even register as a snack. But I wasn't in the Upper Realm. I was stuck here, in this dreary lower plane, where the tea tasted like boiled grass and the entertainment consisted of watching sect leaders grovel. A 20 wasn't a feast. It was barely a sip of watered wine. But it was *something*.

Stealing his luck, piece by piece, would be a game. A distraction. The joy of it would be roughly equivalent to sipping a regular cup of tea back in my own realm—nothing to get excited about, nothing to write home about. But this backwater didn't even have regular tea. The swill they served here, which they probably considered a heavenly treasure, was an insult to my palate. So I would make do. I would take this idiot's paltry 20 luck the way one might accept a mediocre cup of tea: not satisfying, but better than staring at the wall.

Lily sobbed at my feet, her voice rising in desperation. "Please! Please, I'm begging you, Young Master! Save him and I swear on my soul I will serve you for the rest of my life!"

The serpent of flame was inches from the idiot's chest.

I raised my hand and snapped my fingers.

The sound was quiet, almost delicate. It shouldn't have carried over the roar of the flame technique. But the moment my fingers met, a ripple of absolute authority pulsed outward from my position. The crimson serpent froze mid-lunge, its fanged maw open barely a finger's width from the idiot's heart. For a single, crystalline instant, the entire hall was a painting—every figure locked in place, every sound swallowed by my will.

Then the flame dissolved.

It didn't explode or disperse. It simply unraveled, thread by crimson thread, until there was nothing left but a faint wisp of smoke and the lingering scent of charred ozone. The elder disciple who had launched it staggered back as if struck, his face draining of all color.

Silence crashed down like a hammer.

I rose from my seat. My robes, embroidered with the Doe family crest in thread spun from celestial silk, barely rustled. I stepped past the prostrate Lily, past the tea set she had been using to serve me moments ago, and walked toward the idiot.

He was still on the ground, staring up at me with a mixture of confusion and undimmed defiance. His body trembled from the backlash of his forced breakthrough, but his eyes still burned. Twenty luck. It pulsed around him like a fragile heartbeat, invisible to everyone in this hall but me.

I did not look at him.

My gaze stayed fixed ahead, on the grand doors at the far end of the hall, as I passed him. My footsteps echoed on the jade. Everyone held their breath. The idiot's head turned, tracking me, his mouth opening to speak.

I spoke first. My tone was casual, as if ordering a servant to fetch that disappointing tea.

"Throw him in jail."

The words hung in the air. Lily made a choked sound. The sect leader sputtered, "Y-Young Master, as you command! Guards! Take the traitor to the holding cells immediately!"

Rough hands seized the idiot. He didn't resist—his body had nothing left to give. As they dragged him away, I caught a glimpse of his face, twisted in confusion and betrayed hope. He had thought, for one glorious second, that the powerful young master had been moved by Lily's plea. Fool.

The grand doors swung open before me. I stepped into the corridor beyond without a backward glance. Behind me, chaos reigned—the sect leader shouting orders, Lily weeping, the elder disciple probably contemplating whether to flee the sect entirely.

All of it was noise.

My mind was already elsewhere. Twenty luck, trapped in a jail cell, waiting for me. It wasn't much. It was, frankly, pathetic by any civilized standard. But in this realm of zeroes and threes, a 20 was a rare jewel buried in a dung heap. Draining it would give me something to do during my stay here—a small, mild pleasure to offset the utter tedium of the lower realm.

Like a cup of mediocre tea on a boring afternoon. Not good. But better than nothing.















The guest quarters were an insult dressed in silk.

I stood in the center of the room and let my gaze drift across what this sect had proudly presented as their finest accommodations. The ceiling beams were carved from Thousand-Year Spirit Oak—a wood so common in the Upper Realm we used it for servant quarters. The floor tiles hummed with a weak warming array that flickered every third breath, unable to maintain a consistent temperature. The incense burning in the corner was cloying and crude, probably made from ground-up low-grade spirit herbs that some elder thought smelled "refined." A painting of a mountain hung on the wall, the brushwork acceptable but utterly soulless.

My luggage had already been arranged by servants I couldn't remember and didn't care to. A few trunks of clothing, a jade box of tea from home—the only thing in this realm worth consuming—and a small incense burner of my own, which I activated with a flick of qi. True sandalwood smoke began to thread through the air, burning away the local stench.

Behind the main room, a bath had been drawn. Steam curled over the edge of a copper tub large enough for two. Petals floated on the surface—some local flower, probably chosen for its fragrance. They had tried. I almost pitied them for how far short they had fallen.

I undressed without hurry, folding my outer robe and draping it over a chair that creaked under the weight of the celestial silk. The bath water was hot. Acceptably so. I sank into it and let my head rest against the rim. For a long moment, I simply breathed. The new memories, the old memories, the idiot's face, Lily's tears—I let them all settle into neat compartments in my mind, categorized and stored.

This lower realm was a trial of patience. But I had a task to complete.

My father, in his younger years—wild and unchained, before he became the patriarch who made ancient monsters tremble—had lost a spear. Not just any spear. The Sky-Piercer, a weapon he had forged from the fang of a fallen dragon and tempered in the heart of a dying star. In a battle against a rival a thousand years ago, the spear had been knocked from his grasp and cast through the void between realms. It had fallen somewhere in this backwater lower plane, its signature too weak for the family to pinpoint. For centuries, it had been presumed lost. Then, three months ago, a fluctuation. A whisper of the spear's aura, flickering somewhere in this region.

My father, in his infinite and annoying wisdom, had decided this was the perfect task for his only son. "A small errand," he had called it. "Stretch your legs in the lower realm. See how the mortals live. Build character."

Build character. As if I were a child who needed tempering.

I sank deeper into the water until it lapped at my chin. The task was simple: locate the Sky-Piercer and retrieve it. The difficulty lay in the fact that this realm was vast and spiritually barren, the spear's signal buried under a thousand years of mundane filth. I would need to wait for another fluctuation. In the meantime, I was trapped here, drinking swill and sleeping in a room that smelled faintly of mildew.

At least I had found a distraction.

When the water began to cool, I rose, dried myself with a towel woven from spirit silk—my own, not the rough cotton the sect had provided—and dressed in a simple inner robe. Then I walked to the center of the room and raised my hand.

"Elder Shen. Attend me."

I didn't shout. I didn't project qi. I simply spoke his name into the air, and the air obeyed.

Space split.

A seam of darkness tore itself open in the middle of the room, a vertical wound in reality that widened into a portal. From that pocket dimension—a sliver of folded space the size of a small room, where he waited in constant vigilance—a figure emerged.

Elder Shen of the subordinate Yuen family. He was old in the way mountains were old, his face weathered into stern lines, his beard a waterfall of white that fell to his chest. His robes were midnight blue, simple but immaculate. His cultivation base was suppressed, coiled tight inside him like a dragon sleeping, but even suppressed, it radiated a pressure that would have made this sect's elders weep blood if he released it fully. He had been assigned to my protection since I was an infant. His sole purpose in existence was to ensure no harm came to the heir of the Doe family.

He bowed. Not the shallow bow of an elder to a junior, but the deep, formal bow of a guardian to his charge. "Young Master."

"Shen." I settled into the room's only acceptable chair—a carved piece of spirit wood that creaked but held. "Any news?"

He straightened. His eyes, sharp as honed jade, met mine. "The Sky-Piercer remains silent. My scouts have spread across the continent and found nothing. The fluctuation that drew us here has not repeated. It may be weeks. It may be months."

I nodded, unsurprised. A thousand-year-old lost weapon didn't simply reveal itself because it was convenient. "Continue the search. Widen the net. Check not just spiritual signatures but old legends—tales of a divine weapon, a mountain that glows at night, any folk nonsense. The spear has been here long enough to become a myth."

"As you command." He hesitated, a flicker of something unreadable in his expression. "Young Master, I heard about the incident in the hall. A disciple who broke through mid-combat. And a girl."

News traveled fast. Of course it did. "You'll be watching them for me."

Elder Shen's brow furrowed. "The boy is in jail. The girl is confined to her quarters by her father. They are no threat."

"I didn't say they were a threat." I leaned back, steepling my fingers. "I said you'll be watching them. The idiot especially. Follow him when he's released. Observe his movements, his training, the people he speaks to. Note every detail. I want to know if he visits any hidden places, speaks to any unusual entities, or carries any objects of note. Jewelry, for example. Something he never removes."

The old guardian's eyes sharpened with understanding. "You suspect he has a hidden backer."

"I suspect he's interesting." I allowed a small smile. "And I am very, very bored. Indulge me."

Elder Shen bowed again. "It will be done." He stepped back toward the still-open portal, then paused. "Young Master, if the boy proves to be more than he appears…"

"Then I will handle it." My voice was calm. Absolute. "You're here to watch, not to act. Understood?"

"Understood."

He stepped into the darkness, and the portal sealed behind him with a soft whisper, leaving no trace that it had ever existed.

I sat alone in the silence of the too-small room, the too-weak warming array flickering against the chill. My fingers tapped a slow rhythm on the armrest.

A protagonist. The idiot was almost certainly a protagonist, which meant he had advantages beyond mere luck. In the stories from my modern memories, such figures always possessed hidden benefactors—an ancient master sealed in a ring, a divine beast slumbering in their soul, a technique manual from a forgotten era. Something that whispered secrets in their ear and accelerated their growth beyond all reason.

If the idiot had such a backer, and I simply killed him, the backer might flee. Might seek revenge. Might attach itself to some other idiot and start the cycle anew. Wasteful. Inefficient. No, the cleaner method was to sever the bond first. To turn the hidden master against the student, or the student against the master. Then the idiot would be truly alone, and his luck would flow freely into my cup.

It required delicacy. The right words at the right moment. Gifts presented with just the right amount of implication. A beautiful female soul trapped in a necklace, for instance—offer her a body, a new life, in exchange for a small betrayal. Not outright. Just a seed of doubt. A hesitation at a critical moment. Enough to start the fracture.

I rose from the chair and walked to the window. Outside, the sect grounds were bathed in silver moonlight. Somewhere below, in a dark cell, a boy with 20 luck was probably staring at the ceiling, wondering what had gone wrong. Somewhere else, a girl with 15 luck was crying into her pillow. And somewhere, perhaps very close, a hidden master was watching over its precious student, unaware that a predator from the Upper Realm had just marked them both.

Tomorrow, I would begin. A word here. A gift there. The slow, patient work of unraveling destiny itself.

It wasn't the Sky-Piercer. It wasn't even a particularly good cup of tea. But it was something to do in this wretched, disappointing lower realm.

And who knew? Perhaps when the idiot's luck was mine and his hidden master was swayed to my side, I would even find myself mildly entertained.



















The jail was exactly what I expected. A squat stone building behind the main hall, warded with arrays so crude I could have unraveled them with a sneeze. Two disciples stood guard at the entrance, trembling when they saw me approach. One dropped his spear. The other forgot to bow until I was already past him.

I didn't bother acknowledging either.

The interior was damp. Torches guttered in iron sconces, casting jittering shadows across walls streaked with old moisture. The cells were carved directly into the mountain's bedrock, barred with spirit-iron that had rusted at the edges. A rat scurried across my path and died the moment its heart sensed my presence. I stepped over it.

The idiot was in the last cell. The deepest one. The one reserved for traitors.

He was sitting against the far wall when I arrived, arms draped over his knees, head hanging low. His robes were torn at the shoulder where the guards had gripped him. Dried blood still crusted the corner of his mouth. His cultivation had stabilized at early Core Formation—the breakthrough holding, barely—but his body was paying the price for forcing it. He looked wretched. He looked beaten. He looked exactly like a protagonist in the second chapter of his story, before the hidden master revealed the secret technique that would change everything.

Around his neck, barely visible beneath his torn collar, a simple leather cord. At the end of it, a pendant. A small, unassuming piece of pale jade, carved in the shape of a crescent moon. It didn't glow. It didn't pulse with power. It hung against his chest like a sentimental trinket, utterly unremarkable.

I smiled.

"I see you've made yourself comfortable."

The idiot's head snapped up. His eyes—red-rimmed but still burning with that insufferable fire—locked onto me. "You." His voice was hoarse. "What do you want? Come to gloat?"

I didn't answer. I didn't even look at him. My gaze drifted across the cell, taking in the damp stone, the rat droppings, the single moldy blanket they'd thrown in the corner. It was a prison fit for an animal. The sect had, for once, done something appropriate.

I stepped closer to the bars, my hands clasped loosely behind my back. The idiot tensed. His fists clenched. But I wasn't studying him. My eyes had found the pendant again. The way it sat so innocently against his chest. The way the jade caught the torchlight—just slightly wrong, a hair too deep in its refraction. A piece of jade that cheap shouldn't drink light like that.

"Twenty years," I said, as if musing to myself. "Twenty years of life, and this is where you've ended up. A cell. A beating. Your precious Lily bawling on the floor while you were dragged away like garbage." I let a soft, almost pitying sigh escape my lips. "Pathatic, really."

"I'm not afraid of you." The idiot pushed himself to his feet, swaying. His voice shook with fury. "You can lock me up. You can torture me. But I won't bow to someone like you. Someone who treats people like things."

I still didn't look at him. My gaze remained fixed on the pendant. "Brave words. Hollow, but brave." I tilted my head. "Tell me, boy. What do you think happens next? Do you think you'll break out of here? Do you think you'll grow stronger and defeat the evil young master? Do you think Heaven itself will reach down and lift you from the mud?"

He faltered. Confusion flickered across his face. He had expected threats. Mockery. He didn't understand why I was asking questions that sounded almost like the setup to a joke.

I stepped back from the bars, turning slightly, as if I'd lost interest in him entirely. My voice dropped, softer now, almost conversational. "It must be exhausting. Carrying the hopes of a dead thing on your shoulders. A flame that was once bright, now nothing but embers, clinging to a vessel that can barely hold a spark."

The idiot stared at me. "What are you talking about?"

I wasn't talking to him.

"The body is so fragile at this stage, isn't it? Core Formation." I let the words hang in the damp air. "How long would it take to cultivate a proper vessel? Decades? Centuries? And in the meantime, the soul weakens. Flickers. Every day a gamble against oblivion." My eyes drifted to the torch on the wall, watching the flame gutter. "Could she even hold out that long?"

A flicker. So brief it was almost imperceptible. The pendant shuddered against the idiot's chest. Not a glow. Not a pulse of power. Just a tiny, almost imperceptible vibration—the kind a soul might make when it flinched.

The idiot hadn't noticed. His face was still twisted in confusion and anger. "Are you insane? What are you even saying?"

Finally, I turned to look at him. My expression was perfectly blank. The face of a man who had seen an insect on the path and was deciding whether to step on it or walk around.

"I'm saying nothing at all." My tone was light. "Just thinking aloud. The lower realm makes me philosophical. So much wasted potential. So many blind creatures stumbling toward their deaths without ever understanding why." I turned away from the cell. "Rest well, boy. You'll need your strength."

"Wait." His voice cracked. "What did you mean? Who are you talking about?"

I kept walking. The shadows swallowed me as I moved toward the entrance.

Behind me, the idiot grabbed the bars. "Wait! Answer me!"

The guards scrambled to open the outer door. I paused at the threshold, letting the first pale light of dawn brush across my face. Without turning, I spoke one last time.

"A crescent moon. Such a nostalgic shape. In the Upper Realm, we used to say it carried the souls of fallen cultivators. The ones too stubborn to disperse." I glanced back over my shoulder, just barely, just enough for my profile to catch the torchlight. "Of course, that's just a fairy tale. Isn't it?"

I stepped out into the morning.

The door slammed shut behind me, cutting off the idiot's shout. The guards bowed so low their foreheads nearly scraped the ground. I walked past them without a word, heading toward the guest quarters, my mind already cataloging what I'd learned.

The pendant held a soul. Female. Once powerful, if she could still cling to existence after what was clearly a very long time. Old enough to recognize the crescent moon symbolism. Old enough to know the Upper Realm's fairy tales were often more true than myth. She had flinched when I mentioned the fragility of the body. She had flinched when I asked how long she could hold out. She was weakening. She was desperate. And now she knew that someone in this realm could see her—could speak to her—could offer her something her current host never could.

A body. A real body. Not a fragment of jade, not a whisper in a boy's ear, but flesh and blood and a future.

The seed was planted. I had said nothing directly. I had made no offers. But she was old and clever, and she would spend the next days turning my words over in her mind. She would look at the idiot—her precious student, her vessel, her only hope—and she would start to doubt. Is he progressing fast enough? Is he strong enough? Will he ever be able to create a body for me? Or am I wasting what little time I have left on a boy who might die in the next sect conflict?

Doubt was a poison. It worked slowly, but it worked. And when the time came, when I finally extended the offer outright, she would be ready to listen.

I reached my quarters and poured myself a cup of tea from the jade box I'd brought from home. The aroma filled the room, clean and sharp, washing away the stench of the jail. I sat by the window and watched the sect come alive below—disciples shuffling to morning training, elders gliding toward the main hall, servants scurrying like ants.

Somewhere down there, a girl with 15 luck was probably staring at her ceiling, wondering if the boy she loved would survive. And somewhere, in a dark cell, a hidden master was beginning to question her choices.

I took a sip of my tea. It was excellent. The only excellent thing in this entire realm.

Tomorrow, I would visit Lily.
