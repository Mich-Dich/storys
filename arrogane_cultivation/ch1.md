The moment the first shard of foreign memory stabbed into my mind, I closed my eyes.
It was not pain—merely a flood, a sudden deluge of a life that should not have existed and yet did. A life of glass towers, glowing rectangles, a world without spiritual energy. A man who shared my name, my face, my soul, shuffling through twenty-five meaningless years with a heart too dull to feel ambition and too limp to grasp power. John Doe. Me. The same fundamental imprint, stripped of all that made existence worthwhile. In that gray world, he was nothing; here, I had been sculpted into perfection. The memories settled like sediment in a pond, and I watched them with detached curiosity, unbothered by the whimper of a mortal ego that had once thought empathy was a virtue.
I was still digesting when the sect leader’s oily voice cut through the quiet hum of the hall. My body had not moved from the seat of honor they had given me, a chair of polished spirit-jade on a dais overlooking a sea of bowing ants.
“—and thus, to seal the eternal friendship between the Azure Sky Sect and the exalted Doe family of the Upper Realm, I offer my daughter, Lily Vance, as your Dao companion. A humble gift, unworthy of your notice, but offered with all sincerity.”
Beneath my closed eyelids, I felt the stir of a young woman’s panic. Lily. A name like a soft petal, fitting for livestock of decent breeding. Her cultivation was laughable—late Foundation Establishment—but her spiritual root seemed pure enough. She might serve tea adequately.
Then a voice rang out, shrill and dripping with self-righteous fire. “Sect Master, you cannot! Lily isn’t a tool to be gifted away! This is no alliance, it’s a fire pit, and I will not stand by while you sacrifice her!”
Ah. A moth throwing itself at the sun. I kept my eyes shut, savoring the final threads of memory integration while the hall erupted in scandalized murmurs. The speaker was a young disciple, likely an outer one; his aura had been so faint earlier I had not bothered to register it. Now his spiritual energy flared with desperation. How quaint. He had feelings for the girl. The way his voice cracked on her name told me everything.
For a heartbeat, none of the elders moved. They were waiting, afraid to offend me. Then a colder, smoother voice spoke from near the dais. Senior Disciple Kael, a man of late Golden Core cultivation, who had been standing rigid as a sword ever since I arrived. “You dare insult the honored guest? The Doe family’s grace is not a fire pit, you brainless cur. Let me teach you what happens to those who bite the hand that feeds them.”
I sensed the gathering of energy, the tightening of spiritual threads as Kael launched a palm strike meant to cripple. Wind screamed. Spiritual pressure rolled across the hall. The other low-level disciples flinched.
And then—a dull thud.
The idiot blocked it. Not dodged, not stumbled—he planted his feet and raised one arm, and Kael’s strike shattered against a suddenly surging aura like water on stone. The shock that rippled through the hall was almost as delicious as a fine wine. Gasps, choked cries, the scrape of elders rising from their seats. The idiot’s cultivation, which a moment ago had barely brushed Foundation Establishment, now roared at early Nascent Soul. A hidden expert? A fortuitous encounter? The classic mold: a trash young master, a ring with an old grandpa’s soul, a sudden reversal. In the Upper Realm, such stories were a copper a dozen. Here, they were enough to turn the local ants’ world upside down.
Kael stumbled back, face white. The idiot stood trembling, fists clenched, his Nascent Soul aura flaring like a torch in a barn. “I won’t let anyone take Lily away!” he shouted, eyes blazing toward me.
Cute.
The last memory fragment clicked into place. The life of Modern John was fully catalogued, a quaint footnote. I inhaled slowly, the taste of lower-realm spiritual energy thin and unrefined on my tongue, and opened my eyes.
The hall swam into focus: high alabaster pillars, banners of azure silk, a crowd of rigid cultivators in robes of blue and white. The idiot was a young man with ordinary features, sweat on his brow, standing protectively in front of the dais like a farmer’s dog barking at a dragon. Senior Disciple Kael had retreated two steps, his right hand shaking. Sect Master Alistair Vance, a Void Refinement cultivator who could snuff out the idiot with a thought, was watching me, frozen between the desire to crush the insult and the terror of offending me further. His daughter Lily stood half-hidden behind his ceremonial chair, her delicate face pale, fingers twisted in her sleeves. She kept throwing the idiot tiny, stricken glances. They had not even held hands, I could tell, but her heart was wailing.
I did not look at the idiot. I did not acknowledge Kael. Their fight was a speck of dust on my sleeve.
My teacup was empty.
I turned my head slightly, and my gaze fell on the girl. “Lily,” I said. My voice was quiet, but in the silence it carried like a heavenly decree. “Pour me more tea.”
She flinched. Her large, water-bright eyes jumped from the idiot to me, then to her father, who gave a tight, desperate nod. With trembling steps, she approached the table at my side, lifted the jade teapot, and refilled my cup. A tiny splash betrayed her nerves. The tea’s fragrance rose, a mediocre spirit-leaf blend from a backwater realm, but warm.
I lifted the cup, breathed the steam, and did not drink. Instead, I let my gaze drift to the young Nascent Soul fool still standing in the center of the hall, chest heaving. Then I looked back at Lily, at the almost invisible quiver in her lower lip.
“Do you want to rescue that idiot?” I asked, tone mild as a lazy afternoon.
Her breath caught. The entire hall heard it. Her fingers tightened on the teapot until her knuckles went white. She knew what I was offering, and she knew it was not kindness. It was a transaction. A curiosity. A test to see what sort of livestock she would prove to be.
I smiled faintly, the expression not reaching my eyes. “Speak.”









Lily’s lips parted. Her eyes, those trembling pools of water, flicked once toward the idiot—still standing in the center of the hall with his pitiful Nascent Soul aura flaring like a candle in a hurricane—then back to me. A single tear slid down her cheek. She did not bow. She did not plead. She simply lifted her chin, frail as a frost-touched blossom, and gave the only answer that mattered.
“Yes. I will sell myself. Spare him.”
The idiot screamed her name. Lunged forward. Sect Master Vance’s spiritual pressure smashed down on him before I even lifted a finger, pinning him to the marble floor like a crushed insect. The sound his bones made was satisfying. Lily flinched but did not move from my side. Good livestock.
I set my teacup down with a soft click.
“Accepted,” I said.
The word dropped into the silence like a blade. Lily’s breath shuddered out of her. The idiot writhed against his invisible restraints, cursing, tears streaming down his unremarkable face. I paid him no more mind than a farmer pays a bleating sheep destined for the butcher. He was alive, and that was the extent of my generosity. The girl had offered herself; I had taken the trade. Simple. Clean. My teacup was still warm.
But something else stirred beneath my skull, a flutter of curiosity inherited from that gray, ambitionless life. The idiot’s outburst, his hidden cultivation, his improbable defiance—the pattern was so familiar it almost bored me. In John Doe’s world, they had a name for this. *Protagonist.* A child blessed by the narrative itself, destined to stumble into treasures, to rise against all odds, to gather friends and lovers like a lodestone gathers iron. If this lower realm were a story, the idiot was its main character. And I had just stolen his heroine.
The thought amused me more than it should have.
I raised two fingers, a lazy gesture that required almost no spiritual energy, and tapped the air before my eyes. A technique surfaced from the depths of my vast repertoire—*Heaven’s Providence Sight*—a method so esoteric that even Immortal Kings would weep blood to glimpse it. The mortal plane dissolved into threads.
Fortune Threads. The shimmering, intangible weave of Heaven’s favor that clung to every living soul. Some called it luck, some called destiny; I called it a ledger, and I had learned to read its columns when I was still a child in the Upper Realm.
The first thing I saw was myself.
Even prepared, I had to narrow my perception instantly. My own fortune was a sun—no, a supernova—a raging, incandescent glory of golden-white threads so dense and radiant that the very star this lower world orbited was a mere candle drowned in noon. The threads coiled around me like a living galaxy, humming with the weight of a name that had shaken heavens. Beautiful. As beautiful as ever. I allowed myself a heartbeat of satisfaction, then dimmed my sight until my radiance became tolerable and I could observe the insects.
The cultivation world measures fortune in *Chains.* A single Chain of Fortune is roughly the favor required to stumble upon a low-grade spirit stone. Ten Chains might earn you a fortuitous encounter with a minor legacy. A hundred Chains could birth a regional overlord. A thousand… well.
I scanned the hall.
The outer disciples flickered with paltry light. Five Chains. Seven. One particularly wretched girl had three, her threads frayed and tangled like old yarn; she would die young and forgotten. The inner elders ranged from ten to twenty, their fortune threads thin but steady, enough to maintain their stagnant positions. Sect Master Alistair Vance boasted a respectable twenty-two Chains, each strand robust and proud—he had reached Void Refinement for a reason, after all.
Then I looked at Lily.
Thirty Chains. They wove around her slender frame in delicate, silvery braids, pulsing gently with unspoken potential. Not prodigious, but notable. A perfect supporting character for the true hero. Her destiny, I knew with absolute certainty, had once been to stand at the idiot’s side, to heal his wounds, to be the emotional anchor that let him break through every bottleneck. That future had ended the moment she poured my tea.
And then—the idiot himself.
Forty-five Chains.
I almost smiled. The threads did not merely hang around him; they *danced.* They coiled and spiraled with a vitality I rarely saw, vibrant gold edged with the faint purple of Heaven’s regard. A fortune this dense in such a backwater realm meant only one thing: he was exactly what I suspected. A Heaven’s Chosen. A protagonist in the making. His path would be littered with tribulations that refined rather than destroyed him, with masters who appeared at the brink of death, with ancient inheritances that unlocked at precisely the right moment. In a thousand years, if no one interfered, he would ascend to the Immortal Realm with vengeance in his heart and a small army of jade beauties at his back.
How *tediously* predictable.
But also—how amusing.
A stray memory drifted up from the mundane life: the concept of a slow-burn tragedy, where a hero’s light was extinguished not by a single blow but by a thousand tiny cuts. I had always been low in empathy, even in that world, but high in… appreciation for the gradual. The methodical. Torture as a passtime had been one of my few genuine pleasures across both lives. And here, before me, stood a canvas still wet with hope.
I decided then what I would do during my stay in this backwater realm. The mission that had brought me here—a trivial errand to retrieve something that had been buried for ten millennia—would require patience. The seal would not weaken for some weeks yet. I had time. And time, without entertainment, was a sin.
I would not kill the protagonist. That would be crude. Quick. A waste of narrative potential. No—I would *chip* at his luck. Slowly. Gently. One Chain at a time, so imperceptibly that even Heaven itself might fail to notice the theft. I would unspool his destiny strand by strand and weave it into my own already blinding sun, or perhaps simply let it dissolve into nothing. Watching a fortune’s chosen struggle, fail, and crumble into mediocrity, never understanding why the world had suddenly turned against him—that was a vintage worth aging.
A week without a fortuitous encounter. A month without a breakthrough. A rival who should have been defeated now matching him blow for blow. A secret realm that closed its gates just before he arrived. A mentor who died one breath before imparting the final technique. Each event a single thread, plucked.
Lily shifted beside me, still holding the teapot, her thirty Chains brushing softly against my arm. She had no idea what she had just agreed to. To her, I was simply a tyrant who had taken her freedom. She could not comprehend that she had just been severed from a narrative that would have made her immortal. Pity? No, I felt nothing of the sort. She was livestock that had chosen its master. That was all.
I dismissed the Providence Sight and the world returned to flesh and stone. The idiot lay pinned, still glaring at me with those burning eyes. Forty-five Chains of hatred, simmering. Delicious.
“Release him,” I said to Sect Master Vance. The command was soft, almost bored.
The sect master hesitated, sweat beading on his forehead. “Young Master Doe, he insulted you gravely. I must—”
“I said release him.” My tone did not change. The temperature in the hall dropped nonetheless.
Alistair Vance lifted his spiritual pressure. The idiot gasped, dragging himself to his knees. Lily made a tiny sound, half-sob, half-relief. The idiot stared at her, then at me, and I saw the promise of death etched into his soul. *I’ll kill you. I’ll become stronger. I’ll take her back.* The words were unspoken, but they hung in the air like cheap incense. All protagonists swore the same oath. None of them ever realized they were merely acting out a script written by forces they could not see.
Forces that I could now edit.
I rose from the spirit-jade chair. The hall collectively held its breath. My robes, even plain by my standards, shimmered with the subtle embroidery of an Immortal King’s dynasty, and the ants parted as I stepped down from the dais. Lily followed a half-step behind, as was proper. Her father opened his mouth, perhaps to remind her of filial duties, then wisely closed it.
As I passed the kneeling idiot, I paused. Did not look at him. Did not need to. I spoke one line, quiet enough that only he and Lily could hear, and laced it with a wisp of my true cultivation—not the suppressed Earth Immortal aura I had worn since descending, but a sliver of something far beyond. A taste of the word *Venerable* whispered against his soul.
“Grow strong, little protagonist. I want this to be interesting.”
Then I walked on, leaving him trembling in a puddle of his own shattered pride, his forty-five Chains of fortune ripe for the harvest.
Behind me, Lily’s footsteps followed. Her tears fell silently on the marble. The sound was sweet.




















The guest house sat atop a peak called Jadecloud Summit, where the spiritual veins of the entire Azure Sky Sect converged like capillaries into a heart. I stepped through its outer gate and felt the local qi thicken by perhaps a factor of three—dense enough that a mortal would suffocate, dense enough that an outer disciple might weep with joy to cultivate here for a single night. To me, it was breathing through gauze.
The courtyard was paved with flawless spirit jade, each tile carved with minor gathering arrays that glowed faintly blue. A waterfall of liquid spiritual essence cascaded from a floating island above, feeding a lake stocked with thousand-year koi that swam in lazy, shimmering circles. Pillars of white dragonwood, the kind lower-realm kings would trade cities to possess, held up roofs tiled with phoenix-feather glaze. The sect had spared no expense. They had built a palace fit for an immortal, by their pathetic standards, and they had probably congratulated themselves for it.
I had summer homes in the Immortal Realm built from collapsed stars, where the foundation was compressed neutron matter and the walls were woven from the frozen light of dying nebulae. The qi inside those estates was so thick it had achieved sentience in at least three instances; a mortal cultivator from this realm, even one at Void Refinement, would not merely drown in it—his meridians would liquefy, his golden core would detonate, and his nascent soul would scream itself into nonexistence before the first breath finished. This guest house, by comparison, was a mud hut with gold paint.
I did not sigh. Sighing implied disappointment, and disappointment implied expectation. I had none.
Lily followed three steps behind and to the left, exactly as she should. Her footsteps were so light they barely disturbed the spirit-mist curling around our ankles. She had stopped crying at some point during the walk—I had not been paying attention—and her breathing had steadied into the controlled rhythm of a cultivator suppressing her emotions. Her thirty Chains of fortune shimmered faintly even without the Heaven’s Providence Sight active, a whisper of destiny now tethered to mine. She was, for all practical purposes, my property. The sect leader had been explicit; she had been explicit; Heaven itself had registered the transfer. And yet, her heart remained with the idiot.
That was the problem.
Taking her physically removed a pillar of his support. But true fortune theft, the kind that gnaws at a protagonist’s foundation, requires more than separation—it requires *defection*. Her luck was woven into his. If she pined for him, if she dreamed of rescue, if every night she clutched her robes and whispered his name to the uncaring stars, then some fragment of that bond remained. A single thread of loyalty can hold a world. I needed to sever every thread, and I could not do it with force. Love cannot be crushed; it must be starved, redirected, replaced. The girl must choose me not because she had no choice, but because she forgot she ever had one.
That would take time. Weeks, perhaps months. Coincidentally, the exact window I needed to wait.
We reached the bathing chamber. The doors were twin slabs of ice-jade carved with a mural of nine dragons chasing a pearl, each scale etched by a formation master who had probably spent a century on the work. They swung open at my approach, revealing a pool the size of a small lake, steaming with vapor so thick it condensed into rainbow droplets on the ceiling. The water was not water—it was liquefied spirit essence, refined a hundred times, infused with twelve rare herbs that screamed their medicinal value into the very air. For this realm, it was a treasure that could advance a Core Formation cultivator by an entire minor stage in a single soak.
For me, it was lukewarm tap water.
I stepped inside. Lily hesitated for a fraction of a breath, then moved to follow. I felt her intention before her foot lifted—she had been trained for this, or had accepted it, or both. A Dao companion’s duty includes attendance. She would bathe me. Oil my hair. Perhaps offer the other services that livestock offered their masters in quiet rooms.
The door slammed shut in her face.
I did not turn. I did not explain. I simply let the silence stretch as I undid my outer robe and let it fall to the jade floor. On the other side of the ice-jade, I sensed her confusion—a ripple in her spiritual energy, a stutter in her heartbeat. She stood there, frozen, one hand half-raised as if to knock, then slowly lowered it. She did not understand. Good. Understanding was a luxury I would dole out in portions so small she would not realize she was starving until she no longer remembered the taste.
I descended into the pool. The spirit essence closed over my skin like a suggestion of moisture, utterly inadequate to cleanse pores that had been reforged in the heart of a collapsed star. I submerged my head, then surfaced, letting the droplets slide off my hair without wetting it. The bath was a prop, a performance. The real cleansing would happen in my thoughts.
I lay back against the pool’s edge, arms spread along the jade rim, and stared at the ceiling where a painted dragon stared back with gemstone eyes. My mind drifted.
The spear.
Ten thousand years ago, give or take a mortal century, my father had fought a battle in this realm. I did not know the details—he did not share such trivia, and I had never been curious enough to ask. What mattered was that he had lost a weapon. Not his primary weapon, not even a particularly important one; a spear he had carried in his youth, before he became what he was now, before he ascended beyond the title of Emperor and into the realm where names become concepts. The spear had been shattered in the clash and scattered across the fabric of this lower plane. The shaft had been recovered long ago. The tip remained.
Recently—recently by immortal standards—a pulse had been detected. A faint emission of the spear’s residual aura, leaking from whatever seal or crevice had hidden it for ten millennia. The family’s diviners had narrowed the location to this particular region of this particular lower realm. The first pulse had been a flaring match in an infinite darkness, just enough to mark a direction. But for a proper retrieval, a second pulse was needed. The spear tip was not merely buried; it had been sealed, deliberately or accidentally, behind layers of broken space and temporal distortion. Ripping it out with brute force would collapse the local spacetime into a singularity that would swallow the realm. Patience was required. The second pulse would come, in weeks or months, and it would pinpoint the exact coordinates.
Until then, I waited.
Waiting did not bother me. I had cultivated in seclusion for centuries at a stretch; a few months were a single breath. But this lower realm was tedious. The qi was thin. The food was ash. The people were ants performing a pantomime of civilization. The only flicker of interest so far had been the idiot, and even he was merely a familiar pattern I had seen play out a thousand times in a thousand realms. Protagonists rose and fell like waves. I had outlived enough of them to find the cycle nostalgic.
And yet.
My thoughts circled back to the spear, and to a problem I had not yet solved: how to retrieve it without shattering the realm. The second pulse would give me the location, but the act of claiming it was another matter. I was suppressing my cultivation to the Earth Immortal stage, and even that was a lie told through a dozen layers of seals and limiters. If I exerted too much, if I accidentally let a fraction of my true power slip, if I drew upon my fortune with anything resembling intent—the consequences would be catastrophic. Not for me. For the realm. The lower plane was a soap bubble; I was a forging hammer. Even my passive luck, if I aimed it deliberately at finding the spear, would warp causality in ways the realm’s fragile weave could not withstand. Heaven’s Chains would snap. Space would tear. The sun I called fortune would burn this world’s destiny to cinders.
I needed a bloodhound. Someone whose luck was potent enough to sniff out the extraordinary, but whose existence was too insignificant to strain the realm’s fabric. Someone who, if fate bent around him, would merely look like a rising genius rather than a cosmic anomaly. Someone Heaven itself had already chosen as its narrative instrument.
The idiot.
The thought unspooled with satisfying elegance. Protagonists did not merely stumble into treasures—they were *drawn* to them. Their forty-five Chains of fortune were a compass needle that pointed toward opportunity, danger, and ancient legacies. If I could guide him without his knowledge, point his protagonist instincts in the right direction, he would lead me to the spear tip with all the earnest diligence of a trained hound. He would think it was his own idea. He would believe he had discovered a power that would let him challenge me. And when he finally unearthed it, trembling with triumph and the first taste of hope, I would simply take it from him.
The theft of a treasure was also the theft of luck. A protagonist who fails to claim his destined weapon is a protagonist whose fortune Chains begin to crack. The spear would serve two purposes: my mission and my entertainment.
Lily, too, had her role. Separating her from the idiot was only step one. Step two was making her care for me, or at least cease to care for him. I had no illusions that she would fall in love—love was a mortal sickness I had never caught and never wished to—but loyalty could be cultivated like a garden. Attention, protection, small kindnesses that cost me nothing but would loom large in her limited experience. The slammed door tonight was not rejection; it was the first note of a melody she had never heard. She expected me to use her body. When I did not, she would be disoriented. Disorientation opened cracks. Through those cracks, I could pour whatever poison I chose.
Tomorrow I would be courteous. The day after, distant. Then, a gift. Then, a moment of apparent vulnerability. The idiot would fume and train and swear vengeance, and each day she did not think of him, a Chain would weaken. Each night she fell asleep in *my* guest house, under *my* protection, a thread would snap. By the time he found his spear, she would be a stranger to him, and he would be too broken to notice.
I opened my eyes. The steam had thinned. The bath’s lukewarm spirit essence was beginning to cool, its medicinal properties already exhausted. I rose, water sheeting off skin that had not truly been touched by it, and stepped onto the jade floor. A robe of living silk—something the guest house had provided, woven by mortal hands and pathetic beyond measure—draped itself over my shoulders.
Through the door, I sensed Lily still standing there. She had not moved. Her spiritual energy was a knot of confusion, anxiety, and a stubborn, flickering ember of hope that she had not yet identified as misplaced.
I smiled, the expression a cold crescent.
“Lily,” I said, not raising my voice. The sound carried perfectly through the ice-jade. She started.
“Yes, Young Master?” Her voice was small, but steady. She was trying hard. Commendable.
“You will sleep in the adjacent chamber tonight. Tomorrow morning, prepare the tea yourself. I find the servants here… inadequate.”
A pause. She was searching for the trap in my words. “As you command.”
“Good.” I turned away from the door, toward the window that overlooked a sea of clouds tinted silver by the moon. The idiot was somewhere out there, nursing his wounds and his hatred, already plotting his first step on the ladder of revenge. I could almost taste his forty-five Chains, bright and golden and utterly defenseless.
“Sleep well,” I added, and the words dripped with a gentleness I did not feel.
Her confusion deepened. I felt it like a drop of ink in clear water. Perfect.
The spear would wait. The second pulse would come. Until then, I had a protagonist to cripple, a girl’s heart to unstitch, and a lower realm to endure without breaking it by accident. The mission was trivial. The entertainment, however, was proving to be a rare vintage indeed.
















The morning tea was adequate.
Lily had prepared it with her own hands, as commanded, and I noted that her hands no longer trembled when she poured. She had spent the night in the adjacent chamber—I had sensed her restless sleep through the walls, her spiritual energy flickering with dreams of a boy she was trying very hard not to mourn. When she entered my room at dawn, her eyes were dry and her posture was rigid with a new resolve. She was adapting. Livestock always did. The ones that didn't had the decency to die quickly.
I sipped the tea. It was brewed from Silver Vein leaves, a local varietal that tasted of mist and thin promises. She watched me with cautious expectation, waiting for criticism or praise. I gave neither. The silence was its own lesson: she was not interesting enough to comment on yet. Her thirty Chains shivered, one thread loosening from its anchor to a distant protagonist.
After I set the cup down, I said, "I will take a walk."
She blinked. "Shall I accompany you?"
"No."
Another confusion, another crack. The door closed behind me, and I left her standing in the guest house with a cold teapot and a thousand unasked questions. The cultivation of loyalty was a slow art, and I had centuries of practice. Today's lesson: absence. Let her wonder where I went. Let her imagination supply the horrors she expected me to commit. When I returned unscathed and indifferent, her relief would be one more brick in the wall between her and her old life.
The mountain path descended through clouds that parted around me like nervous servants. The spirit jade steps were polished to a mirror sheen, reflecting a gray sky that I had learned, in that other life, to call "overcast." The word surfaced from the mundane memories and dissolved, irrelevant. I walked at a mortal pace because rushing was for those with limited time.
At the foot of Jadecloud Summit, where the path met the main sect road, a figure knelt on the stone.
The idiot.
He was a wreck. His robes were the same he had worn yesterday—now dirtied, torn at the knees, soaked with morning dew that had long since turned to mud. His hair, once tied in the neat knot of an outer disciple, hung in tangled ropes around a face that had not slept. The faint scar on his cheek was new; someone, probably a senior disciple eager to curry favor, had left it. His Nascent Soul aura was suppressed, clamped down by a formation circle drawn around him in cinnabar and spirit blood. The circle forced him to his knees, drained his qi, and broadcast his shame to every cultivator who passed. A dozen disciples loitered at a respectful distance, watching with the silent hunger of vultures.
Sect Master Vance's work, no doubt. A gift. See, Young Master Doe, we have punished the one who offended you.
I almost smiled. The idiot had not been kneeling long—perhaps since last night—but the formation was designed for Core Formation cultivators at best. At Nascent Soul, he could have shattered it with an hour of focused resistance. He had not. Not because he was weak, but because he was *enduring*. Protagonists endured. They ground their teeth, counted their humiliations, and stored them as fuel for the blazing revenge that would come ten arcs later. This kneel was not submission; it was a promise.
Pathetic. Admirable. Both words applied, and neither mattered.
I walked past him without a glance. The loitering disciples scrambled backward, bowing so low their foreheads touched the ground. The idiot's head lifted a fraction—I felt the weight of his glare like a warm breeze—but he said nothing. His fists clenched on his thighs. The formation hummed, punishing the movement with a pulse of suppression that made his shoulders buckle. He did not cry out. Protagonist.
I stopped ten paces past him. Not for him. For something else.
A whisper, so faint I might have dismissed it as wind, had I not spent a thousand years learning to distinguish wind from *souls*. It emanated from the idiot—not from his body, not from his dantian, but from a spot just above his collarbone. A tarnished necklace, hidden beneath his robes, pulsing with a sliver of consciousness so fragile it was barely a breath.
I turned.
The idiot flinched. He had seen my face change, or perhaps his protagonist instincts were screaming at him. "You—" he started.
I lifted one finger. His jaw locked shut, sealed by a technique so far beyond his comprehension that his spiritual energy did not even attempt to resist. His eyes bulged. His throat worked. No sound emerged. I had not silenced him with pressure or pain; I had simply told the local laws of causality that his vocal cords were not permitted to vibrate in my presence. The universe, as always, obeyed.
I stepped closer, not to him, but to the necklace. The tarnished metal was a copper alloy, Third Epoch craftsmanship, at least three thousand years old. The pendant was a hollow locket no larger than a thumbprint, and inside—
"There you are," I murmured.
The air shimmered. A wisp of pale light bled from the locket, coiling upward like smoke from a dying candle. It shaped itself into a woman.
She was translucent, her form a watercolor painted on the morning mist. Hair like spilled moonlight cascaded over shoulders draped in robes of an ancient cut, all flowing silk and jade ornaments that had gone out of fashion before the current sect master's great-grandfather had drawn his first breath. Her face was beautiful in the way old paintings were beautiful—elegant, melancholic, and utterly irrelevant to the present. A face that had once commanded respect, perhaps worship, now reduced to a ghost in a trinket around the neck of a kneeling boy.
Her eyes, when they met mine, were afraid.
She knew. Not my name, not my purpose, but she had been a cultivator long enough to recognize the shape of something that stood beyond her comprehension. Her spiritual sense, however degraded, was screaming the same warning her host's instincts had screamed yesterday. *Predator.*
"Body Integration," I said. It was not a question. "Late stage, if I'm not mistaken. You were three heartbeats from Mahayana when you fell. Three thousand years ago, give or take a century."

Somewhere far behind, the idiot was bleeding his way through the Broken Fang Mountains, his thirty-nine Chains flickering with each hard-won victory. His journey would continue, his encounters would multiply, and his fortune would slowly, painfully inch upward. But Lily's two hundred and forty Chains and Seraphina's seventy-three were growing faster—because they were anchored to my sun, while his was a candle fighting against a wind I controlled.
The game was proceeding exactly as planned. And the north promised new amusements.
