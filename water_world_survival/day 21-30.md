
### Day 21

The foils were waiting.

I woke early, fed Ember, and went straight to the workshop. The hydraulic system had been pressurized and sealed since Day 19, its lines running through the hull like arteries, its cylinders mounted in the sockets I had built. What it lacked were the wings themselves.

I opened the upgrade interface and began to design.

The foils would be the most complex fabrication I had ever attempted. Each wing was a precise aerodynamic shape—more like an aircraft's than a boat's—designed to generate lift from the flow of water rather than air. The forward pair would sweep back at a thirty-degree angle, their tips rising toward the surface when deployed, their roots pivoting in the hull sockets. The stern foil would be smaller, a T-shaped stabilizer that would keep the boat level when the bow lifted clear of the water.

I specified every curve, every chord length, every taper ratio. The skins would be aluminium over a titanium spar—the titanium salvaged from the barge's deck fittings, the aluminium from her frame. The pivot bearings would be stainless steel, sealed against salt and pressure. The hydraulic rams would be double-acting, capable of extending or retracting the foils in under ten seconds.

The interface calculated the material requirements. I had just enough. The remaining aluminium plates, the last of the stainless bar stock, the titanium fittings I'd been hoarding. The cloud rose, massive and silver, and when it settled, three hydrofoil wings lay on the deck: two forward, one stern, their surfaces gleaming like polished mirrors.

I installed them one by one. The crane lifted the forward foils into position, and I guided them into their sockets, locking the pivot pins and connecting the hydraulic lines. The stern foil went in more easily, its smaller size making it manageable without the crane. By mid-afternoon, all three were mounted, their rams pressurized, their full range of motion tested and smooth.

When I deployed them for the first time—pressing the hydraulic control in the pilothouse—the wings swung down from their retracted position against the hull and locked into the water with a deep, resonant thrum. The boat lifted slightly, not out of the water, but with a new tension, like a horse sensing the approach of a gallop.

I retracted them and sat at the helm, breathing slowly. They were beautiful. They were functional. They were waiting for a power source that did not yet exist.

One day.

---

The reduction gear was simpler work. The planetary set I had designed for the propulsion pod had been installed with the motor, but the final tuning required attention. I spent the late afternoon adjusting the gear mesh, checking the lubrication channels, and running a series of low-speed tests that sent the *Daydream* gliding in slow circles across the empty sea. The pod steered cleanly, the propeller biting without cavitation, the motor's power translating into smooth, silent thrust.

Ember supervised from the aft deck, the bear held firmly under one paw. She was unimpressed by gears but pleased by the sun.










### Day 22

The finishing touches.

I had been living in a skeleton for four days. The hull was sound, the systems were functional, but the interior was bare aluminium and exposed wiring, the decks were plain metal, and the only soft surface aboard was Ember's cat tree. It was time to make the *Daydream* a home.

I started with the decks. The interface fabricated teak planks from the last of the timber scraps—not real teak, but a composite that looked and felt like wood while being impervious to salt and sun. I laid them across the aft deck, the foredeck, and the pilothouse floor, the seams tight and even, the color a warm honey-gold. The swim platform got the same treatment, its steps now broad and inviting.

The interior came next. The galley received a tile backsplash—small hexagonal tiles in shades of blue and white—and the countertops were resurfaced in a warm grey composite that matched the original *Daydream*'s aesthetic. The bathroom got proper fixtures: a rainfall showerhead, a vessel sink, and cabinetry that closed with soft, satisfying clicks. The workshop was fitted with oak-topped benches and a full LED lighting array that made it feel like a professional laboratory.

The master cabin I saved for last. The bed was already there, but I added built-in nightstands with wireless charging pads, a reading lamp with a warm amber glow, and a set of blackout curtains for the portholes. The walls I paneled in white oak veneer, the same shade as the teak decks, with the blue LED accent strips recessed into the seams. It felt like a stateroom on a superyacht. It felt like a place someone could live for years.

Ember's domain received upgrades too. Her cat tree remained by the pilothouse window, but I added a second platform near the master cabin, and a small scratching post in the galley. The blue teddy bear I washed and mended—one of its ears had come loose during a particularly vicious attack—and presented to her on a small cushion near the heater vent. She sniffed it, circled twice, and sat down with the air of a queen accepting tribute.

By evening, the *Daydream* was finished.

I walked through her spaces one by one, running my hand along the woodwork, feeling the solidity of the construction. The boat was unrecognizable from the humble 14-meter cutter I had woken on twenty-two days ago. She was a ship now—a proper ship, with room to spare and systems to envy and a pair of hydrofoil wings folded against her hull, waiting for the day I could give them flight.

I stood on the aft deck as the sun went down, Ember on my shoulder, and looked at the wake stretching behind us in a long, clean line. The sea was golden, then copper, then indigo. The stars emerged, one by one, cold and bright.

Tomorrow, I would decide what came next. The power problem. The alien ship. The flotilla and its false hope. The fog, still out there, still learning. The monsters, still watching.

But tonight, I had a home.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

*Day 22. Transformation complete. 42m LOA, 7.8m beam, hydrofoils installed but unpowered. Interior finished: teak decks, oak paneling, full galley and workshop. Ember's domain expanded with additional platforms. She has accepted the mended bear as satisfactory.*

*The boat is everything I dreamed. The boat is more. But the foils are waiting, and I still don't know how to make them fly.*

*One problem at a time.*

I closed the notebook and carried Ember to the master cabin. She curled on her pillow, the bear tucked under her chin, and closed her eyes. I lay beside her, listening to the pod's quiet hum, and let the long, slow peace of the finished boat carry me into sleep.

It was magnificent. Eight feet long, easily 400 pounds, its body a perfect torpedo of muscle and silver scales. Ember stood at the pilothouse door, back arched, hissing at the intruder. The blue teddy bear lay forgotten. This was a prey animal beyond her comprehension.

I butchered it on the aft deck, the work taking most of the morning. The fillets filled every container I owned and still overflowed. I would be eating tuna for weeks. But I had a better use for most of it.

The trade offer went up before noon:

**daydream-0001** said: "Trading: fresh bluefin tuna, 400 lbs, professionally butchered and flash-frozen (I have a freezer). Portions available in 20 lb increments. What I want: a working desktop computer. Tower, monitor, keyboard, mouse. Does not need to be powerful, just functional. Will consider partial trades for components."

The response was immediate and, frankly, bewildered.

**fishcutter-0491** said: "A computer? What in the world do you need a computer for? You're on a boat."

**daydream-0001** responded: "I'm on a very large boat with a very stable power supply. I have time. I want to build something."

**battleship-0023** said: "We've got a dozen desktops in the officer's quarters. They were used for navigation training. I'll trade one, complete with peripherals, for 40 lbs of that tuna. Our crew hasn't had fresh fish since Day 1."

**daydream-0001** said: "Done."

The trade materialized on the nav desk: a compact tower, a 24-inch monitor, a mechanical keyboard, and a mouse. The machine was military-grade—rugged, fanless, probably older than some of the survivors—but it was intact. I set it up on the desk beside the chart plotter, running cables to a power conditioner I'd salvaged weeks ago. The monitor flickered to life with a soft blue glow.

Ember jumped onto the desk and sniffed the tower. The warm exhaust fan interested her. She sat down directly in front of it, her fur ruffling in the airflow, and closed her eyes in feline bliss.

"You're going to be a problem," I told her.

She purred.

The USB stick was still in my pocket. I had been wearing the same cargo shorts when I woke on Day 1, and somewhere in the chaos of the first weeks I had never emptied the pockets. The stick was a 64-gigabyte SanDisk, scuffed and salt-crusted, but when I plugged it into the tower, the drive spun up without complaint. On it was a bootable Linux image—Ubuntu, a few years out of date—and a collection of development tools I'd used for hobby projects in the old life. Python. Rust. Some machine learning libraries. An old copy of an AI ethics textbook I'd never finished reading.

I installed the OS, configured the network to talk to the boat's NMEA 2000 bus through a bridge I'd need to fabricate later, and opened a blank terminal.

The autopilot I wanted was not a simple heading-hold. I wanted something that could make decisions. Something that could read the radar, the sonar, the chart plotter, and the fog map, and plot a course that minimized risk while maximizing exploration. Something that could learn. Something that could, eventually, handle the boat as well as I could—and, in some edge cases, better.

I called it *Helm*.

**Day 27**

The initial architecture took shape in a haze of coffee and keystrokes. I had been a decent programmer in the old life—not a professional, but an obsessive amateur who built things for the joy of making them work. The Expanse had sharpened that instinct. I coded with the same focused intensity I'd brought to the hull extension, the same patience I'd used to salvage the *Aurora*'s motors.

Helm's core was a decision tree wrapped in a reinforcement learning framework. The boat's sensor data would feed into a state vector: position, heading, speed, wind, current, fog proximity, territory classification, battery state, solar forecast, recent monster sightings. Helm would evaluate possible actions—turn to course X, accelerate, decelerate, deploy foils, anchor, flee—and select the one that maximized a reward function I spent the entire morning defining.

Safety was the highest weight. Distance from known fog banks, distance from monster territory boundaries, proximity to deep water for escape. Next came exploration: Helm should prefer uncharted areas, new seamounts, potential salvage sites. Then efficiency: minimize power consumption, keep the batteries above a threshold, maintain a comfortable ride for the crew.

The crew, at present, consisted of one human and one cat. I added a "crew comfort" variable that penalized sudden maneuvers and rewarded smooth, predictable motion. Ember deserved a steady floor under her paws.

By evening, I had a prototype running in simulation mode. I fed it the past week's navigation data and watched as it plotted courses that were subtly different from the ones I'd taken—sometimes better, sometimes puzzling, always interesting. It avoided a fog bank I'd skirted too closely. It found a more efficient route through a shallow reef system. It suggested a path through a territory I'd circled around, calculating that the risk was lower than I'd assumed.

It was not yet trustworthy. It was barely functional. But it was learning.

Ember walked across the keyboard at 9 PM and inserted a string of random characters into the reward function. Helm immediately proposed a course that would take us in a perfect circle for six hours. I fixed the code and moved Ember to her cat tree.

"You are a hazard," I said.

She licked her paw, indifferent.

**Day 28**

The sea remained empty. The code grew.

I spent the morning building the hardware bridge that would connect the desktop to the boat's systems—a small interface box I fabricated from electronic scrap and a handful of microcontrollers. The upgrade interface did the precision work, aligning the circuit traces and sealing the housing against salt. I plugged it into the NMEA bus, ran a diagnostic, and watched Helm's sensor feed light up with real data for the first time.

The feeling was electric. The boat was speaking to the machine, and the machine was speaking back. I sat at the desk with Ember on my lap, watching Helm process the radar returns, the sonar contours, the chart plotter's steady scroll. It wasn't controlling anything yet—I'd limited it to passive monitoring—but it was *there*, a silent presence in the circuits, learning every mile.

In the afternoon, I traded 20 pounds of tuna for a used GPU—a battered NVIDIA card salvaged from a wrecked gaming rig, its fan bearing whining but its cores intact. The battleship crew threw in a small solid-state drive as a bonus. I installed both, reconfigured Helm's neural network to use the GPU for training, and ran a new simulation. The speed increase was dramatic. The AI chewed through a month of hypothetical scenarios in ten minutes.

"What do you think?" I asked Ember, showing her the monitor. "Is this the start of something important, or am I just keeping busy?"

She blinked at the screen, then at me, then jumped down to retrieve her bear.

I took that as a vote of confidence.

The days had blurred together—26, 27, 28—three sunrises, three sunsets, an infinity of blue water. The population counter held at 643. The fleets reported progress: the Current Fleet had completed its first full patrol circuit, crossing seven territory boundaries without incident. The eastern scouts had mapped three new zones and were pushing further. The alien ship girl posted an updated territory map, the boundaries sharper now, the watcher symbols tagged with estimated threat levels.

**aliencruiser-0001** said: "The ship has been calm. Quieter than usual. I think it approves of the fleets. Or maybe it's just resting. I still don't know how to read it. But I'm learning."

**daydream-0001** responded: "It's not ready. Give me a few more weeks. But when it is, you'll be the first to know."

The chat had changed since the island. The panic was gone, replaced by a grim, methodical determination. People shared maps and routes and monster sightings with the matter-of-fact tone of weather reports. The Expanse was still lethal, but it was no longer unknown. We were learning its rules.

And I was building something that might one day know those rules better than I did.

On the evening of Day 28, I sat on the aft deck with Ember and the blue bear, watching the sun sink into an ocean of polished bronze. The hydrofoils were folded against the hull, their aluminium skins catching the last light. The propulsion pod hummed beneath us. In the pilothouse, Helm's monitor glowed blue, its code scrolling in quiet, patient loops.

I had not solved the power problem. The foils still could not fly. But I had built something else—a mind, embryonic but growing, that would one day make this boat safer, smarter, more capable. It was a different kind of upgrade. One the interface couldn't fabricate.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

*Day 28. 643 souls. Population stable. No deaths in three days. The fleets are moving. The map is growing. Helm's prototype is running in passive mode, learning from real sensor data. GPU installed for training acceleration. Hardware bridge fabricated and operational.*

I closed the notebook. Ember was already asleep on the pillow, the bear tucked under her chin. I lay beside her, listening to the motor's hum, feeling the boat's gentle roll, and let my mind drift through code and currents and the quiet certainty that, for now, we were exactly where we needed to be.

Tomorrow, the work would continue. Helm would learn a little more. The foils would wait a little longer. And the *Daydream* would keep moving north, into waters no one had ever charted, toward a future I was building one line of code at a time.













### Day 29

The cold came gradually, then all at once.

I noticed it first in the small things—the way my breath began to mist in the pilothouse before the heater kicked on, the way Ember sought out the warmest spots on the cat tree and stayed there, the way the sea changed color from tropical turquoise to a deep, iron grey. The air had a bite to it now, a clean sharpness that tasted of pine forests and snowmelt, even though there was no land in sight.

By mid-morning, I had dug out the wool peacoat I'd found on the barge—the one that had provided fabric for Ember's bear—and wrapped it around my shoulders. It was still too large, but it was warm. I made hot tea instead of coffee and stood at the pilothouse windows, watching the world change.

The islands appeared on sonar first, a cluster of jagged returns rising from a seabed that had been slowly climbing for days. When they broke the horizon, they were unlike anything I'd seen in the southern waters. These were not palm-crowned atolls or sandy spits. They were dark, raw, volcanic—cliffs of black basalt rising sheer from the water, their tops dusted with something that might have been lichen or might have been the first tentative grasp of pine forest. Seabirds wheeled around the cliffs in white clouds, their cries carrying across the water like the voices of ghosts.

Ember pressed her nose to the window and made a low, curious sound. She had never seen land like this. Neither had I.

I throttled back and began to pick my way through the outer islands, the sonar painting a labyrinth of reefs and channels. The water was clear and shockingly cold—I could see the bottom in thirty meters, a landscape of dark stone and pale kelp that swayed in the current like something breathing. The air smelled of salt and iron and the faint, distant sweetness of flowering things.

I was so absorbed in navigating the channel that I almost missed the first breach.

The sea exploded.

A sound like a mountain cracking open rolled across the water, and I spun toward the port window in time to see something impossible rising from the sea. It was vast—longer than the *Daydream* by an order of magnitude—a serpentine body of translucent plates and glittering membrane that lifted clear of the water in a slow, graceful arc. Water cascaded from its flanks in silver curtains. At its apex, a massive dorsal fin unfurled like a sail made of mirrors, catching the pale northern sun and flashing it back in a blaze of light so bright I had to shield my eyes.

The creature hung there for an impossible moment—ten seconds, fifteen—its body suspended against the grey sky like a cathedral window come to life. Then it crashed back into the sea with a thunder that shook the boat, sending a wave rolling outward that lifted the *Daydream*'s bow and set her rocking in the swell.

I stood frozen at the helm, my hands locked on the throttle, my heart hammering. The range finder on the chart plotter told me the breach had been 12 kilometers away. Twelve kilometers, and I had felt the impact like a physical blow.

"Ember," I said, my voice very calm, "get in the safe box."

She didn't argue. She had felt the boat shake. She trotted to the box beneath the command station, the blue bear clutched in her mouth, and settled into her towel nest. I secured the mesh and turned back to the helm.

The monster did not come for us.

It did nothing. The sea where it had fallen was a churning cauldron of white foam, but no vast shape rose from it. No serpentine body arrowed toward the *Daydream*. The sonar showed the creature descending, its massive heat signature fading into the deep. Within five minutes, the sea was calm again.

I waited ten more. Nothing.

Slowly, carefully, I pushed the throttle forward and put distance between us and the breach site. My hands were still shaking. I had seen whales before—blue whales, fin whales, the great cetaceans of the old world. This was not a whale. This was something that made a blue whale look like a bathtub toy. Three hundred meters, at least. Maybe more. And the sail—that vast, mirrored sail—had flashed with a light that felt almost deliberate. A signal. A display. A dance.

I didn't know what it meant. I only knew I wanted to be somewhere else.

I navigated the channel at the best speed I could manage, the foils still retracted, the pod pushing us through the cold water at a steady twelve knots. The islands slid past, dark and silent, their cliffs studded with the white specks of nesting birds. The sonar showed nothing but stone and kelp and the occasional darting shape of fish.

And then, twenty kilometers later, the sea exploded again.

This time the breach was off the starboard bow, perhaps 15 kilometers distant. Another leviathan—or the same one, I couldn't tell—rose from the water in the same slow, graceful arc, its sail unfurling to catch the sun, its body a cascade of silver and glass. It hung in the air for a long, breathtaking moment, then crashed back into the sea with that same world-shaking thunder.

I did not run this time. I throttled back and watched.

The creature did not approach. It did not even seem to notice the small aluminium ship bobbing on the swell. It breached, it fell, it vanished. The sea settled. The birds resumed their circling.

I waited. Twenty minutes passed. Then, further north—another breach. A fourth creature, or the same one moving impossibly fast, rose and fell against the grey horizon. The flash of its sail was visible even at this distance, a star-like beacon that pulsed once, twice, and was gone.

Four breaches in less than two hours. All at a distance. None approaching.

I let out a breath I hadn't realized I'd been holding and unsecured Ember's safe box. She emerged with the bear in her mouth, looking mildly annoyed at having been confined. She jumped onto the nav desk and rubbed her head against my chin.

"I think," I said slowly, scratching behind her ear, "they're not hunting."

She purred. She had no opinion on leviathans.

---

I spent the afternoon observing.

The creatures—I was now certain there were several, perhaps a pod—breached at irregular intervals throughout the day. Always at a distance. Always with that same magnificent, sun-flashing display. They seemed to be following a pattern, moving along the island chain in a loose circuit, breaching, descending, and breaching again. Not hunting. Not threatening. Dancing.

I opened the interface and created a new log entry, separate from my daily journal. I called it *Northern Megafauna Observations*.

*Specimen 001 — Provisional Name: Glass Sail Leviathan (Speculivelum abyssalis)*

*Length: Estimated 300+ meters. Body plan: serpentine, laterally compressed, covered in transparent or translucent chitinous plates. Appears nearly invisible in water column except when breaching. Propulsion: undulating body strokes, powerful enough to clear approximately one-third of body length from water during breach.*

*Most distinctive feature: retractable dorsal crest ("sail") composed of lattice cartilage and a chromatophore-rich membrane. Can shift from transparency to mirror-like reflectivity. During breach, sail is unfurled at apex, catching sunlight in a brilliant flash visible for hundreds of kilometers. Function unknown—hypotheses include parasite removal, surface prey stunning, intraspecies communication, or purely aesthetic display.*

*Behavior: multiple individuals observed breaching in loose circuit around northern island chain. All breaches occurred at distance (12-20 km) from vessel. No aggressive behavior observed. No approach behavior observed. Creatures appear indifferent to presence of small craft.*

*Preliminary conclusion: not hostile. Territorial, but territory does not exclude vessel traffic. Possible that breaches are a regular behavioral pattern unrelated to human presence. Do not approach. Do not provoke. Observe and document.*

I attached a photograph—the best I'd managed to capture during the third breach, the leviathan suspended against the grey sky, its sail a blaze of silver light. It was slightly blurred, the boat's motion defeating the camera's stabilization, but the scale was unmistakable. The creature was beautiful in a way that made my chest ache.

I posted the log and the image to the public chat that evening.

Ember was on the cat tree, the bear under her chin, her eyes half-closed in contentment.

The cold was sharper now, the heater humming in the background. I wrapped the peacoat tighter and stood at the window, looking out at the dark water. Somewhere beneath it, vast and silent, the Glass Sail Leviathans were swimming their ancient circuits. They did not care about me. They did not care about the Expanse, or the game, or the 400-day cycle. They had been here long before the first survivors arrived, and they would be here long after the last one fell.

I was a guest in their world. A visitor. And for the first time since waking on this ocean, I felt something that was not fear or determination or grim satisfaction. I felt wonder.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

Observed new megafauna species: Glass Sail Leviathan (Speculivelum abyssalis). Multiple individuals, 300+ meters, breaching with mirrored dorsal sail displays. Non-hostile. I closed the notebook and carried Ember to the master cabin. She curled on the pillow, the bear under her paw, and I lay beside her, listening to the distant cry of seabirds and the gentle lap of the cold northern sea.

Outside, the leviathans slept in the deep. Outside, the stars wheeled overhead. Outside, the ocean stretched to every horizon, vast and ancient and full of wonders I had only begun to find.

I closed my eyes and let the quiet carry me into sleep.











### Day 30

The idea crystallized while I was debugging Helm's obstacle-avoidance module.

The desktop computer was powerful, but it was also a single point of failure. If the tower died—salt corrosion, power surge, Ember knocking a cup of tea into the motherboard—Helm died with it. I needed redundancy. I needed a fleet of smaller, low-power computers that could run the same code in parallel, sharing the load and backing each other up. And if I could build one, I could build many.

The problem was infrastructure. A modern computer chip required a global supply chain—silicon fabs, clean rooms, photolithography, rare-earth mining. None of that existed in the Expanse. But the upgrade interface didn't need a supply chain. It only needed raw elements and a precise description of the final structure.

I sat at the workbench with a notebook and began to sketch.

A Raspberry Pi was, at its core, a printed circuit board with a processor, memory, and input-output controllers. The processor was silicon doped with precise amounts of boron and phosphorus. The memory was an array of capacitors and transistors. The board itself was fibreglass and copper. Every component could be reduced to a list of elements. If I could provide those elements in sufficient purity, the interface could assemble them directly, skipping the entire industrial base that had made such devices possible in the old world.

The catch was getting the elements. I had copper and aluminium and silicon in abundance. But the dopants—boron, phosphorus, arsenic in microscopic quantities—were harder to source. Gold for the connectors. Tantalum for the capacitors. A dozen other trace elements that I couldn't extract from seawater or salvage from hull plates.

I needed organic material. Living things accumulated trace elements in their tissues—plants, bones, shells. Things that had once been alive held a periodic table's worth of complexity. And I needed gold. Just a tiny amount. A single necklace would provide enough for a hundred processors, atomized and redeposited by the interface.

I opened the trade channel.

**daydream-0001** said: "Trading: fresh bluefin tuna, 20 lb portions. Also available: custom machined parts, aluminium stock, and one slightly used 10 kW electric motor (my original drive unit). Looking for: gold jewelry (any condition, even broken), bone meal, dried seaweed, shells, teeth, antlers, hardwood ash—any organic material with complex mineral content. Also seeking broken electronics of any kind, regardless of condition. Will explain later. Or not."

The response was immediate and confused, as my trades usually were.

**fishcutter-0491** said: "Gold? You want gold? What for?"

**daydream-0001** responded: "Microscopic connectors. I'm building computers."

**fishcutter-0491** said: "Of course you are. I have a gold tooth. It's been in my mouth for ten years. Do you want my gold tooth?"

**daydream-0001** said: "If you're willing to part with it. But jewelry is easier."

**sailboat-1190** said: "I have a gold chain. My grandmother's. It's the only thing I have left from the old world. But I haven't eaten fresh fish in three weeks, and I'm pregnant. The necklace for 40 lbs of tuna. Please."

I paused. A gold chain. A grandmother. A pregnancy. The Expanse had stripped everyone down to their essentials, and sometimes the essentials were heavy with memory.

**daydream-0001** said: "40 lbs of tuna, plus a solar-powered watermaker I fabricated last week. You'll need it. Keep the chain. Send me something organic instead—hair, fingernail clippings, a handful of dried kelp. Anything that was once alive."

**sailboat-1190** said: "Are you serious?"

**daydream-0001** said: "Always."

She sent a lock of hair and a pouch of dried kelp. I sent the tuna and the watermaker. Somewhere on the endless blue, a pregnant woman on a small sailboat was eating fresh fish and drinking clean water, and I had a few grams of carbon and nitrogen and trace minerals that the interface could use.

The gold came from another source: a sailor on a battered catamaran who had a broken wedding ring and no sentimental attachment to it. He took 20 pounds of tuna and a custom winch bracket I fabricated from aluminium scrap. The ring dissolved in the interface's cloud and reappeared as a small ingot of pure gold, no larger than my thumbnail, but enough for a thousand processors.
