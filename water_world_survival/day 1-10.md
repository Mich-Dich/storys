
### Day 1

I woke to the smell of the sea and the gentle rock of a hull I knew by heart.

For a long moment I simply lay still, eyes half-open, letting the familiar lines of the master cabin settle around me. White bulkheads. Soft blue glow from the LED strip along the ceiling cove. The low hum of ventilation. Morning light slanted through the starboard porthole and painted a pale rectangle on the duvet.

This was the bow cabin of the *Daydream*. My boat. The one I had spent three years designing in SketchUp and Rhino, optimizing every hull line, every wire run, every millimeter of living space. The one I built a dozen times in my head while sitting in a dull office, waiting for life to begin.

Only I had never built it. Not in the real world.

I sat up, heart starting to knock against my ribs. The bed was real—I could feel the memory foam yield under my palms, the crisp cotton of the sheets. The gentle pitch was unmistakably a monohull at anchor. Through the porthole I saw only grey water and a pale, overcast sky.

Then a voice spoke inside my skull. Not in my ears. In my head.

*"Attention. You have been relocated."*

I froze.

*"You are one of one thousand individuals selected for the Ocean Expanse. Each of you now occupies the water vessel that most occupied your waking imagination in your prior existence. For some, this was a lifelong dream. For others, a casual fascination. Regardless, that vessel is now your reality."*

A translucent blue panel shimmered into existence at the edge of my vision, like a heads-up display from a video game. It moved as I moved my head, always staying just off-center until I focused on it directly. Text scrolled slowly:

**Population: 1000**

**Status: Active**

**Objective: Survive.**

The voice continued, calm and without warmth.

*"This world is a global ocean. There are no continents. Land exists only as small islands, never larger than five kilometers in diameter. Resources are scattered, finite, and must be gathered. The black fog you will see on the horizon is lethal. Avoid it. It moves. It hungers. Everything else you must discover for yourself."*

A pause. The panel flickered, then expanded slightly, revealing a second pane—a simple, text-based chat log, currently empty.

*"You may communicate with other participants via this interface. You may also access your status, inventory, and map. Use them. Or don't. The Expanse does not care."*

The voice stopped. Silence poured back in, filled only by the lap of water against the hull.

I sat on the edge of the bed for perhaps ten seconds, breathing slowly, letting my engineer's mind chew on the data.

One thousand people. Ocean world. No continents. Lethal fog. The vessel I'd obsessed over for years.

And that fog—I needed to see it.

I stood, still in the T-shirt and shorts I must have fallen asleep in, and climbed the short rise to the command station. The *Daydream*'s interior was designed with a split-level: the master cabin in the bow, then a 0.6-meter step up to the helm and salon area, then back down to the galley and living space aft. It gave the command station a panoramic view through wraparound windows, like a small tower in a floating home.

The helm was exactly as I'd drawn it. A compact console with a twelve-inch chart plotter, throttle lever, and a row of tactile switches for lighting, pumps, and systems. My laptop—my actual laptop—sat on the fold-down desk to the left, its lid closed. Behind me, the L-shaped sofa was upholstered in charcoal fabric, the one I'd picked from a marine catalog and never ordered.

I touched the chart plotter. It lit immediately, showing a nautical chart of an empty ocean, no land in any direction. A depth reading of 2,300 meters scrolled beneath. In the corner, a timer counted down: **Safe passage: 47:58:12 remaining**. Some kind of initial grace period before navigational hazards appeared? Useful.

Then I looked up, out the forward windows.

The black fog was there. A low, churning wall on the southern horizon, darker than any storm cloud I'd ever seen. It didn't rise. It sat on the water like a crouched animal, its surface shifting with slow, oily eddies. Even from miles away, it felt wrong. A primal part of my brain whispered *go*.

I pressed the main power switch. The battery management system woke with a soft beep. State of charge: 98%. Solar input: 0.0 kW—the panels were still in shadow, the sun barely above the opposite horizon. But the bank was full.

I pressed the motor start.

A near-silent whine rose from the stern, and the water beneath the swim platform began to churn. The throttle was a simple lever. I nudged it forward until the power meter read 3 kW. The *Daydream* began to move, her bow cutting north-east, directly away from the fog bank. The chart plotter showed our speed: 4.1 knots.

On the solar monitor, the first trickle of morning sun hit the roof array: 0.3 kW and climbing. I'd be net-positive within an hour.

Satisfied, I let the autopilot hold the course and turned my attention to the interface panel. The chat log was no longer empty.

I sat on the sofa, the boat steadying into her gentle rhythm, and began to read.

A man on a replica pirate ship claimed he had cannons but no cannonballs. Another on a Coast Guard cutter was already trying to organize rescue operations.

I read for almost an hour, saying nothing.

The sun rose fully, and my solar array woke. By 08:00, the panels were pumping 4.1 kW into the batteries while the motor sipped 3 kW. A net gain. The fog bank had shrunk to a dark line on the horizon, slowly losing ground.

I stood and stretched. The boat was running perfectly. The watermaker had auto-started and was filling the tank. The fridge was cold. The induction cooktop blinked a ready light.

Time to explore.

I walked the full length of the *Daydream*, my fingers trailing along the white fibreglass walls, the black anodized aluminum trim. Everything smelled new—fresh paint, clean upholstery, the faint ozone tang of electronics.

From the command station, I stepped down into the living area. To port, the galley: a deep stainless sink, a two-burner induction hob, and a compact fridge with a tiny freezer compartment. The watermaker would handle hydration, but I'd need to think about food eventually.

To starboard, the bathroom. A composting toilet (no plumbing nightmares), a small shower stall, and a mirror cabinet already stocked with basics. I splashed water on my face and looked at myself. Aft of the galley, the workbench. This was my domain. A solid wooden surface with a built-in vise, drawers of hand tools—screwdrivers, wrenches, a multimeter, wire strippers, a fibreglass repair kit, even a compact 3D printer I'd spec'd for fabricating small parts. It was all there. Everything I'd ever bookmarked, ever priced, ever dreamed of having within arm's reach while at sea.

I opened the last drawer. A set of dive fins and a mask. I hadn't included those in my plans—not specifically. But the boat had.

Under the sofa in the command station, I found the safe. A biometric lock. I pressed my thumb to the sensor; it beeped and opened. Inside was empty, but the fact that it existed, that the system had filled in a gap I hadn't fully thought through, unsettled me in a quiet way.

The aft porch was a stretch of teak slats over fibreglass, sheltered by the overhanging solar roof. A swim platform at the very stern sat just above the waterline, with a ladder descending into the sea. I stood there for a while, watching the wake spread behind us, the ocean a uniform grey-blue under the climbing sun.

The boat was 14 meters of calculated perfection. My dream, down to the last bolt.

At noon, I made coffee on the induction hob and took it to the command sofa. The chat was still scrolling.

And I was on a solar-powered liveaboard with infinite range and a fully stocked workshop, sipping espresso and watching my battery tick upward.

I felt no guilt. Only a quiet, profound relief.

By late afternoon, the fog was a memory. The chart plotter's safe passage timer still had 40 hours on it, so I set the autopilot to a gentle zigzag that would keep us moving roughly north-east without straying into the deeper unknown. I didn't know if the fog could change direction, but staying mobile felt right.

I tried to access the other interface functions. The "Inventory" tab showed a sparse list: *Provisions (7 days), Water (40 L tank + watermaker active), Tools (assorted), Personal effects.* The "Upgrade" tab was greyed out, no explanation. Maybe it unlocked later. The "Trade" tab existed but was empty—no active trades. The map showed only my immediate radius, fog marked as a red smear to the south, slowly moving westward.

So. The basics were there, but the system wasn't showing its full hand yet.

Fine. I could wait.

Dinner was pasta with a tin of tomatoes and some dried herbs I found in a cabinet. I ate on the aft porch, legs dangling over the swim platform, watching the wake phosphoresce faintly in the evening light. The sea was empty. No birds. No ships. I set the night cruising mode—1 kW draw, navigation lights on, a gentle 3 knots. The batteries were at 91%, and the solar had tucked away for the night. The wind turbine would pick up any breeze and trickle a little back.

I brushed my teeth in the bathroom, rinsed my face, and climbed back down into the master cabin. The bed felt even more welcoming now, the sheets cool and clean.

Lying in the dark, I listened to the water sliding past the hull, the faint creak of the boat as she rode the gentle swell. Somewhere out there, a thousand people were fighting for their lives. Some were already losing.

But I was not.

I was on the boat I had dreamed into existence, in a world that wanted me dead, and I had never felt more at home.

I closed my eyes. The autopilot would keep us safe. The fog was far behind.

Tomorrow, I would start figuring out what came next.















### Day 2

I woke to the soft chime of the battery monitor announcing a full charge.

For a disoriented moment, I forgot where I was—the gentle rock, the white ceiling, the salt-and-ozone smell—and then memory poured back in a cold, quiet wave. The Ocean Expanse. The black fog. A thousand strangers scattered across an endless sea.

I lay still, listening. The motor was a barely perceptible hum through the hull. The boat was still moving. Good.

I swung my legs out of bed and climbed the step to the command station. Morning light flooded the windows, pale gold on the dark water. The chart plotter showed our position unchanged in essence—an empty blue field with depth readings in the thousands of meters. The safe passage timer had ticked down to thirty-six hours. The fog icon was a red smear far to the southwest, moving slowly westward. No immediate threat.

The battery readout gleamed: 100%. Solar input was already 1.2 kW and climbing as the sun cleared the horizon. The wind turbine had contributed a modest 0.8 kWh overnight, more than enough to cover the 1 kW draw of night cruising. I was starting the day with a full tank of electrons and nowhere to be but here.

I made coffee—the same induction hob, the same stainless cup—and sat at the nav desk with the interface open. The population counter still read 1000, but the number had a faint grey tinge to it, like a shadow. I hadn't noticed that before.

The chat was already scrolling.

I set the coffee down. The words hung in the quiet of the command station, heavier than they should have been for text on a screen. A man named Marcus was dying alone in a cold metal tube somewhere under my feet, and all I could do was read his goodbye.

I didn't know him. I couldn't save him. But I read every word.

A moment later, the population counter flickered.

**Population: 999**

The name *submarine-4358* faded to grey in the chat log and did not send again.

I sat very still, letting the coffee go cold in my hands. The chat went quiet for a full minute, as if the whole world had paused to notice. Then it resumed—some offering condolences, some too wrapped in their own terror to respond, a few already back to bickering over coordinates and salvage rights.

I closed the interface and stood. Grief was a luxury I couldn't afford, but I didn't dismiss it. I filed it. Marcus and his dog Biscuit. He'd wanted a submarine, once. Casually, probably. A passing fascination. The Expanse had given it to him and buried him with it.

I walked aft to the workbench and found a small, unused notebook in one of the drawers—a waterproof Rite-in-the-Rain pad, exactly the kind I'd bookmarked and never bought. I wrote on the first page:

*Day 2. Marcus (submarine-4358). Remember.*

Then I turned the page and started a proper log. Power stats. Weather observations. Coordinates. I needed data, not emotion. Data would keep me alive.

The morning passed in methodical routine. I checked every system: watermaker output steady at 30 liters per hour, batteries balanced, solar array free of salt spray. I deployed the small sea anchor I'd found in a locker and used it to slow our drift while I opened the hatch to the motor well and inspected the electric drive. It was pristine—a compact, water-cooled 10 kW unit, its casing barely warm to the touch after 24 hours of continuous operation. The propeller was clean. The seals were tight.

I ran a diagnostic on the navigation electronics. Radar, sonar, GPS—all nominal. The chart plotter was slowly building a bathymetric map of the seafloor beneath us, revealing a featureless abyssal plain that dropped to over 3,000 meters. Nothing to salvage there, even if I had the gear.

By mid-morning, I turned my attention to food. The galley had a basic rod and reel stowed in a long locker beside the swim platform, along with a small tackle box of lures. I'd planned for fishing—it was part of the liveaboard fantasy—but I'd never actually specified the gear. The system had filled it in.

I set the throttle to idle, letting the boat drift just enough to satisfy my internal rule about never being truly stationary, and cast a line off the stern. The water was clear and impossibly blue, sunlight slanting down into depths I couldn't fathom. For twenty minutes, nothing. Then a sharp tug, and I reeled in a small, silvery fish I didn't recognize. It looked like a mackerel but wasn't. I photographed it with the interface's image function—maybe someone in chat could identify it—and cleaned it on the swim platform, tossing the guts to the sea.

Lunch was fresh fish seared on the induction hob with a squeeze of lemon powder from the spice rack. I ate on the command sofa, scrolling the chat with one hand.

The chat was a tapestry of slow-motion disaster, and I was a spectator in a comfortable seat. I didn't feel guilty—guilt required responsibility, and I hadn't put these people here—but I felt something. A weight. An awareness that my quiet, sunlit morning was an anomaly in a world of rising dark.

I posted nothing. I offered no advice. The fishcutter seemed competent, and the battleship crew was learning. The rest would figure it out or they wouldn't. My interference wouldn't change the fundamental arithmetic of the Expanse.

Instead, I spent the afternoon mapping.

The interface's map function was more sophisticated than I'd initially realized. It allowed manual annotation—I could draw routes, mark waypoints, and overlay data from the sonar and radar. I began building a personal chart, marking the fog's position and movement vector, the coordinates of every island or shallow area mentioned in chat, and the rough locations of other survivors who had shared their positions. Most were scattered across the map in a loose cluster—we'd all started in the same general region, give or take a few hundred miles.

I also noted the fuel barrel sightings. There were more than I'd expected. The fishcutter had marked three in the past day alone, all floating in the same current line. The trawler had found a cluster of five barrels lashed together near a small atoll. It seemed the Expanse provided—but unpredictably, and never enough for everyone.

By late afternoon, I had a rough picture of our starting area. An open stretch of deep water, bordered by shallow seamounts to the north and east, with a scattering of tiny islands beyond. The fog was a slow-moving predator, sweeping from south to west, herding us toward the shallows. Whether that was intentional or just physics, I couldn't say.

I set a new course: north-northwest, toward a seamount the fishcutter had mentioned. It was shallow enough for diving, and he'd hinted at wreckage on the bottom. If I was going to survive long-term, I needed more than fish and sunshine. I needed metal, electronics, materials to build with.

The upgrade tab in the interface was still greyed out, but I had a feeling it wouldn't stay that way forever. The system had given us tools but not instructions. It wanted us to figure things out.

As the sun set, painting the water in shades of copper and rose, I made another meal—rice with the last of the fresh fish—and settled onto the aft porch. The sea was glassy calm, the only sound the whisper of the hull cutting through the water. Far to the south, I could just make out a dark line on the horizon—the fog, or a cloud bank. I marked it on the chart anyway.

The population counter sat at 994. Six people had died since I woke. I hadn't seen any of them, but their names were grey in the chat log, their stories already fading.

I thought about Marcus. About the girl on the floating island, crawling along at one knot. About the cruise ship passengers in their lifeboats, hoping for an island that might not exist.

And then I thought about my boat. My quiet, efficient, beautiful boat, humming through the night on a river of sunlight.

I didn't know why I'd been given this chance while others were drowning, suffocating, drifting toward the fog. I didn't believe in fate or fairness. But I believed in preparation, and I had prepared for this life without ever knowing it would become real.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

*Day 2. 994 souls. Fog movement consistent with current patterns—predictable for now. Seamount salvage planned for tomorrow. Watermaker flawless. Batteries full. Mental state: steady.*

*The world is trying to kill us. I am not cooperating.*

I set the night cruise—1 kW, 3 knots, navigation lights on—and climbed into the master cabin. The bed welcomed me like an old friend. Through the overhead hatch, I could see stars, sharp and cold in a moonless sky.

Somewhere out there, a jet ski was empty and drifting. A submarine was a silent tomb. A floating island inched through the dark.

But the *Daydream* sailed on, and so did I.

















### Day 3

The morning brought a gift: the seafloor rising to meet me.

I woke at dawn, checked the batteries—98%—and poured coffee before even glancing at the chart plotter. The screen showed a gentle incline in the seabed, depth decreasing from 2,800 meters to just under 40. The seamount the fishcutter had mentioned was real, and I was drifting over its outer slope.

I throttled down and let the *Daydream* glide to a near-halt, the motor just barely turning over at 0.5 kW to maintain steerage. The water was impossibly clear; I could see the reef below, a landscape of pale coral and dark volcanic rock, dotted with small, darting fish.

The safe passage timer had expired overnight without fanfare. I was now in a world with real topography, and I intended to use it.

I finished my coffee, pulled on a pair of board shorts, and retrieved the dive mask and fins from the workbench drawer. The air was warm, the sea calm. I set the autopilot to hold a slow circle around the seamount's summit—not quite anchored, but close enough for a quick dive—and stepped onto the swim platform.

The water was cool against my skin as I slid in, a welcome shock. I took a breath, ducked my head under, and looked down.

Twenty feet below, the reef fell away in a tumble of boulders. And there, wedged between two coral heads, was a hull. A small runabout, maybe eighteen feet, its fibreglass skin cracked but mostly intact. It had been down here a while—seaweed clung to the gunwales, and a patina of green fuzz covered the outboard—but it was salvage.

I surfaced, grabbed a line and a mesh bag from the deck locker, and dove again.

The work was slow and rhythmic. I pried loose four aluminium cleats, their screws corroded but the metal sound. I unbolted a stainless steel grab rail from the bow and hauled it up in stages, letting the buoyancy of the empty air tank I'd found in the runabout's locker help with the lift. In a storage compartment under the console, I found a handheld GPS unit, its screen clouded with salt but its circuit board potentially intact. I bagged it carefully and kicked back to the surface.

By mid-morning, I had a small pile of salvage on the aft porch: aluminium, stainless steel, and one thoroughly soaked piece of electronic scrap.

I toweled off and checked the interface. The Inventory tab had updated automatically.

**Resources Acquired:**  
- Aluminium: 4.2 kg  
- Stainless Steel: 3.8 kg  
- Electronic Scrap: 1 unit

I stared at the list for a moment, then noticed something else. The Upgrade tab, which had been grey and inert since my arrival, was now lit with a soft blue outline.

So. The system wanted me to have materials before it would show its hand. That made sense, in a game-logic sort of way. You don't unlock crafting until you have something to craft with.

I tapped it open. The interface shifted to a blank schematic window, overlaid with a prompt: *Design desired upgrade. Specificity reduces resource cost.*

I thought for a moment. My first instinct was something practical—a wind generator, maybe, to supplement the solar at night. But I'd need magnets and copper for that, and my salvage pile was mostly aluminium. Better to start small, test the system's logic before committing to anything ambitious.

I sat at the nav desk, pulled out my notebook, and sketched.

Fishing rod holder. Simple. A bracket to mount on the stern rail, angled to hold a rod while trolling. I'd designed dozens of brackets in my workshop over the years; this was muscle memory. I specified the dimensions—three inches wide, two inches deep, with a cutout for the reel foot—and focused my mind on the image.

The interface flickered. A schematic appeared, cleaner than my sketch, rotating slowly in 3D. Beneath it, a list:

**Required Resources:**  
- Aluminium: 0.3 kg  
- Stainless Steel (fasteners): 0.1 kg

**Cost reduction due to design detail: 87%**

I let out a low whistle. The system wasn't just a vending machine—it was a collaborative design engine. The more precisely you knew what you wanted, the less it demanded. A vague idea was expensive. A blueprint was cheap.

I confirmed the fabrication.

The aluminium and a small piece of the stainless rail on the porch dissolved, not with heat or noise but with a soft shimmer, like heat haze over asphalt. The particles rose in a silent cloud, hung in the air for a heartbeat, then flowed together into a solid shape. The rod holder dropped gently onto the deck, still warm to the touch, every edge deburred and anodised a matte black.

It took maybe twenty seconds.

I picked it up. The weight was perfect, the mounting holes pre-drilled to fit the stern rail exactly. I bolted it on in under a minute, slid a fishing rod into the slot, and gave it a tug. Rock solid.

I grinned, alone in the vast blue silence, and let myself enjoy the moment.

Then my engineer's brain kicked in again.

The rod holder was good. But I could make it better. What if it didn't just hold the rod? What if it told me when a fish struck?

I had electronic scrap. A waterlogged GPS unit, but inside it would have a small speaker—for waypoint alerts—and possibly a pressure sensor if the buttons were solid-state. Even if not, I could repurpose the circuit board.

### Day 8

The plateau appeared on sonar just after ten in the morning, a broad underwater mesa rising from the abyssal plain to depths of only fifteen to twenty-five meters. I throttled back and circled the area, watching the bottom contour paint itself across the chart plotter in shades of blue. The water was clear enough that I could see the seafloor from the deck—a pale, sandy expanse dotted with dark patches of weed and the occasional glint of metal.

I had been traveling north since dawn, aiming for a cluster of shallow banks the battleship's mapping team had shared the previous evening. The Expanse was slowly becoming charted, not by any central authority, but by the accumulated fragments of a thousand survivors, each contributing what they saw. I'd added my own observations—fog movements, monster territories, good salvage sites—and taken from the common pool. It was a barter economy of information, and it worked.

This plateau wasn't on anyone's map yet. It was mine to discover.

I set the autopilot to a slow circle and stood at the rail, scanning the bottom. The water was astonishingly clear—the sun high enough to pierce the surface and illuminate the seabed in shifting lattices of light. Small fish darted through the weed. A ray, broad and flat, glided away from the boat's shadow.

And there, half-buried in the sand near the edge of a dark patch of rock, was a barrel.

A big one.

I grabbed my binoculars and leaned over the side. The barrel was a standard 55-gallon drum—250 liters, give or take—its metal skin patched with rust but intact. It lay on its side, partially silted in, with the unmistakable shape of a sealed bung fitting visible on the top end. It had been down here a while, but not long enough to corrode through.

If it was full, and if the contents were still good, it was a treasure. Fuel, oil, chemicals—anything in that quantity was worth more than gold in the Expanse. And even if it was empty, the steel alone was valuable.

I had to get it up.

The pressure bottle was still at half charge from yesterday's salvage, and I had the flotation bag. But a 250-liter drum full of liquid could weigh over 200 kilograms. The single bag might not be enough, and even if I got it to the surface, getting it aboard would be a struggle without a proper crane.

I needed more lift. And I needed a plan.

I spent an hour preparing. At the workbench, I opened the upgrade interface and designed two more flotation bags, slightly larger than the first, with reinforced attachment straps and quick-inflate couplings. I used the last of my fibreglass repair cloth, some nylon webbing from a spare ratchet strap, and a few steel rings I'd salvaged from the runabout's hardware.

**Required Resources:**
- Fibreglass Cloth: 1.5 sq m
- Nylon Webbing: 3 m
- Stainless Steel Rings: 4 units

I confirmed. The cloud rose and settled, and two new bags lay folded on the bench, each the size of a small duffel when deflated.

Next, I rigged a pulley system. I didn't have a davit crane yet—that was the next major project—but I had the electric windlass on the anchor, and I had a collection of blocks and cleats from my spares. I set up a temporary A-frame using the stern rail and a length of steel pipe I'd been saving, lashed it tight with line, and mounted two pulleys—one at the top of the frame, one at the base. I ran a heavy line from the windlass drum, through the pulleys, and down to the water, where it ended in a steel carabiner.

It wasn't elegant. It probably wasn't safe. But it would lift a barrel if I could get it to the surface.

By noon, I was ready. I stripped to shorts, pulled on my mask and fins, and slipped into the water.

The sea was warm and quiet, the only sound my own breathing and the distant hum of the *Daydream*'s motor at idle. I dove, pulling the deflated flotation bags behind me, and made my way down through the sunlit water to the barrel.

It was even bigger up close. A heavy-gauge steel drum, its surface rough with rust and speckled with tiny barnacles. I cleared sand from around the bungs—both were sealed tight, the gaskets swollen but intact. I rapped the side with my knuckles. The dull thud of a full container echoed back.

Diesel. Or maybe oil. Either way, heavy.

I wrapped the first flotation bag around the barrel's middle, cinching the straps tight. The second bag went near the end, the third at the other end, creating a balanced lift. I connected all three to the pressure bottle's hose, said a silent prayer to the gods of salvage, and opened the valve.

The bags billowed. Air rushed in, silver bubbles streaming upward, and the barrel shifted. Sand billowed around it. For a long, breathless moment, nothing happened—and then, slowly, majestically, the barrel began to rise.

I kicked upward beside it, guiding it away from the rocks. The bags swelled to full size just below the surface, three silver pillows straining against their straps. The barrel broke the surface with a gentle splash and bobbed there, buoyant and docile.

Now came the hard part.

I climbed back aboard, attached the pulley line to the barrel's lifting straps, and engaged the windlass. The motor whined. The line went taut. The A-frame creaked, and for a terrifying second I thought the whole contraption would tear free of the rail—but the lashings held. The barrel lifted clear of the water, swaying gently, and I guided it over the gunwale with a boat hook. It settled onto the aft deck with a solid, satisfying thud.

### Day 9

Outside, the ocean stretched to every horizon, dark and deep and full of teeth.

But in here, there was warmth and fur and the quiet hum of a boat that ran on starlight and dreams. I closed my eyes and let the sea carry us both toward morning.





















### Day 10

The morning began with Ember discovering the litter box.

I had set it up in the bathroom the previous afternoon—a simple tray of sand from the shallows, cut low so her tiny legs could manage the climb. She had ignored it completely, preferring to explore the workbench and sleep in a parts tray. But when I woke at dawn, she was not on my pillow. I found her in the bathroom, patting the sand with a look of intense feline concentration, and when she finished, she meowed at me with unmistakable pride.

"Good job," I said, and meant it. She had solved a problem on her own. That made her, in some small way, a proper ship's cat.

After breakfast—minced fish for her, coffee and oatmeal for me—I settled into the command station to plan the day's work. The solar array was performing beautifully, the morning sun already pushing 6 kW into the system. I'd stopped worrying about battery levels days ago; the *Daydream* was a floating power plant, her reserves perpetually full, her energy budget in permanent surplus. I could run every system aboard indefinitely and still have power to spare.

The next logical step was to expand the battery bank anyway. Not because I needed more range—I already had that—but because I wanted more *burst* capacity. A larger bank could feed a more powerful motor for longer sprints, turning the sleeper speedboat concept from a party trick into a genuine tactical advantage. If I ever needed to outrun a fog bank or cross a monster territory at speed, I wanted the reserves to do it.

I opened my inventory and reviewed my stocks. Lithium cells, copper bus bars, battery management modules. I had salvaged a handful of cells from the flooded battery bank two days ago, and I'd accumulated enough copper to wire a small substation. What I lacked were high-quality cathode materials—the rare-earth compounds that made modern lithium cells energy-dense and stable. I could fabricate them from raw ore, but I had no ore. I needed a trade.

I was composing a request in the chat when a private message arrived.

**Anne** said: "You're the one who traded a rod holder for copper wire on Day 6. I've been watching. You fabricate things—don't bother denying it. I need a custom part, and you need lithium cells. I have twenty pristine 100Ah LiFePO4 prismatics. More than enough for whatever battery expansion you're planning. Interested?"

I sat very still, my fingers hovering over the keyboard. The message was direct, informed, and unsettlingly specific. She knew about the rod holder trade. She knew I was expanding my battery. She had used the word *fabricate*—not a common term in the chat, where most people still talked about "finding" or "salvaging" gear.

This was not a normal survivor.

**daydream-0001** said: "What custom part?"

**Anne** said: "A high-pressure micro-inverter, 48V DC to 240V AC, pure sine wave, rated for continuous 3kW. Waterproof to IP68. My unit failed last week and I can't run my watermaker without it. I have the specs. You have the skill."

I read the message twice. The part was specific—an advanced piece of power electronics. Most survivors wouldn't know what a micro-inverter was, let alone need one. And she had lithium cells, pristine ones, in quantities that suggested she'd been salvaging for a long time.

I probed.

**daydream-0001** said: "That's a very specific request. And you seem to know a lot about what I need. How long have you been here?"

There was a pause. Long enough that I thought she might have ended the conversation. Then:

**Anne** said: "As of 10 days ago, officially one year. Four hundred and ten days exactly. I arrived with the cycle before yours. There are a few of us—not many. Most died in the first month. The ones who lived learned to pay attention. I've been paying attention to you."

One year. Four hundred days.

I leaned back in my chair, the implications cascading through my mind. I had assumed—we had all assumed—that the thousand people in the chat were the only survivors, that the Expanse had started on Day 1 with a single cohort. But Anne was from a previous cycle. She had been here for more than thirteen of our months, surviving the fog, the monsters, the attrition that had killed over a hundred and fifty people in a single day.

And if there were previous cycles, there would be future ones.

**daydream-0001** said: "You said a few of you. How many cycles have there been?"

**Anne** said: "I know people who've been here eight hundred days. Two years. They're... different. Cautious. They don't talk much, even to me. Beyond that, it's all rumors and ghost stories. Someone claims to have met a three-year survivor, but I've never seen proof. The ocean gets harder the longer you stay. Things change. The fog learns. The monsters get smarter. Most people don't make it past the first monster season."

**daydream-0001** said: "Monster season?"

**Anne** said: "The four-day attacks are just the start. There are cycles within cycles. But that's not a conversation for chat. You want the lithium cells or not?"

She was closing the door, deliberately. I let her. Pushing too hard would spook her, and I needed those cells.

**daydream-0001** said: "Send me the inverter specs. I'll build it."

She sent a detailed specification sheet—more detailed than most engineers would bother with. Voltage curves, thermal tolerances, connector types, even the preferred brand of MOSFET. I recognized the precision of someone who knew exactly what they needed and why. She was not just a survivor; she was a technician, probably an engineer in her previous life. Like me.

I designed the inverter in the upgrade interface, matching her specs exactly. The cost was steep—rare-earth magnets, high-purity copper, electronic scrap—but I had everything except the magnets, which the interface extracted from a small servo motor I'd salvaged from the runabout's steering system. I activated the fabrication, watched the cloud do its silent work, and a compact, beautifully machined inverter materialized on the desk.

**daydream-0001** said: "Done. Sending trade request: inverter for lithium cells."

**Anne** said: "That was fast. You're better than I thought."

**daydream-0001** said: "You're not the only one who pays attention."

She accepted the trade. The inverter vanished, and in its place, a stack of twenty pristine lithium cells appeared on the deck, each one gleaming in its factory wrapping, their terminals protected by plastic caps. They were beautiful. They were worth a fortune.

**Anne** said: "One piece of free advice, from someone who's been here a year: the four-day attacks are a tutorial. The real threats take months to notice. Patterns in the fog. Changes in the water. Things that hunt by sound, not by stillness. I've seen boats vanish that did everything right—moved constantly, never stayed still—and they still died. No one knows why. The two-year veterans might know, but they don't share."

**daydream-0001** said: "Why are you telling me this?"

**Anne** said: "Because you're useful. You build things. If you die because you didn't know the rules changed, I lose a supplier. Consider it an investment."

**daydream-0001** said: "Fair enough. Happy anniversary."

She didn't respond. But I had the sense she was still there, somewhere in the endless blue, watching.

I sat for a long time after the chat closed, processing what I'd learned. The 400-day cycle explained why Anne seemed so knowledgeable—she'd had a year to learn. It also meant that in three hundred and ninety days, a new wave of survivors would arrive, fresh and panicked and utterly unprepared. The chaos of Day 1 would repeat. The monster attacks of Day 4 would repeat. A thousand new people would flood the chat with the same questions, the same desperation, the same slow winnowing.

And somewhere in that new wave, someone would be on a ship they couldn't operate—a battleship, a submarine, an alien cruiser—and they would need a way out. I filed that thought away. No plan yet. Just a possibility.

The second piece of information was more troubling. Anne had hinted at threats beyond the four-day timer—dangers that took months to manifest. The fog "learning." Monsters getting smarter. Boats vanishing despite following all the rules. She didn't have answers, only warnings. That meant the Expanse was not a static puzzle to be solved once and then mastered. It was an evolving threat. The game changed as you played.

I looked at Ember, who had returned to her parts tray and was washing her face with the unhurried contentment of a creature who understood exactly zero of this.

"You're lucky," I told her. "You only have to worry about fish and naps."

She blinked at me, which I took as agreement.

I spent the afternoon on the battery expansion. The twenty lithium cells from Anne, combined with the salvaged cells I already had, gave me enough capacity to build a 40 kWh bank—double what I'd started with. I designed the new battery modules in the interface: compact, water-cooled, with integrated charge controllers and a modular rack system that would mount in the expanded lazarette beneath the aft deck. The fabrication consumed most of my remaining copper and electronic scrap, but the result was worth it.

When the cloud settled, the new battery bank was installed and online, its status LEDs glowing a steady green. The power display updated: 40 kWh total capacity, enough for eight hours of cruising at 5 kW, or a one-hour sprint at 40 kW if I upgraded the motor to match. The *Daydream* was becoming a wolf in sheep's clothing—a quiet, solar-powered liveaboard that could, if necessary, outrun almost anything on the water.

I tested the system with a brief acceleration run, pushing the motor to 15 kW—a draw the old bank would have struggled to sustain. The boat surged forward, her bow lifting, her wake flattening into a clean, fast channel. 10 knots. 11 knots. The motor didn't even warm up. I held it for five minutes, then throttled back, satisfied.

Ember, who had been sleeping on the command sofa, opened one eye at the sudden motion and closed it again. She was unimpressed by speed. She was a cat.

In the evening, I anchored near a small atoll and sat on the aft porch with Ember in my lap, watching the sunset. The sky was a furnace of orange and pink, the sea a mirror of fading gold. I had been in the Expanse for ten days, and I had a larger boat, a full battery, a growing reputation, and a kitten who purred when I scratched behind her ears.

But I also had new knowledge, and it sat heavy in my chest. The 400-day cycle. The long-term threats. The veterans who didn't share their secrets. Anne had given me a gift wrapped in a warning: the Expanse was more dangerous than I knew, and the only way to survive was to keep learning, keep moving, keep building.

I was good at all three.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

*Day 10. 812 souls. First contact with a previous-cycle survivor. Designation: Anne. Time in Expanse: 410 days. Confirmed: new survivors arrive every 400 days. Some veterans have survived 800+ days, but they are rare and secretive. Threats escalate over time—fog adapts, monsters grow smarter, unknown factors cause disappearances even among mobile vessels. *Battery bank expanded to 40 kWh using lithium cells from trade. Motor upgrade next on the list. The sleeper speedboat concept is working: quiet and efficient at cruise, fast when needed. Ember continues to be excellent crew.*

*The Expanse is not a death sentence. It's a long, slow test. I intend to be one of the veterans who passes it.*

I closed the notebook and carried Ember to bed. She curled into her usual spot—the hollow of my shoulder—and began to purr, a tiny engine of contentment in the dark.

Outside, the ocean stretched to every horizon, ancient and patient and full of teeth. But I was patient too. I had time. I had a boat that grew stronger every day. I had a cat who thought I was the safest place in the world.

And I had a year—more, if I was careful—to figure out the rest.
