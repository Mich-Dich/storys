
### Day 11

The wreck was not on any chart.

I found it by accident, chasing a faint magnetic anomaly on the sonar that I'd initially dismissed as a rock formation. But the shape was wrong—too angular, too regular—and when I throttled back and circled, the shadows beneath the water resolved into the unmistakable lines of a hull. A big one. Fifty feet, maybe sixty, lying on its side in thirty meters of water at the edge of the plateau.

I anchored at a safe distance—still well within my 48-hour window—and stood at the rail with binoculars, trying to piece together what had happened. The vessel had been a power catamaran, sleek and modern, its twin hulls still vaguely intact. But something had torn through the port side with catastrophic force. The bridgedeck was shattered, the aluminium frame twisted outward like a peeled fruit. The starboard hull was largely intact, but the port hull was barely recognizable as a boat.

And the water around it was still stained faintly brown, as if the violence had happened recently.

I felt the old prickle of caution. A wreck this fresh might still have monsters nearby. But the sonar showed no large contacts, and the current was carrying the sediment away, dispersing it into the blue. Whatever had done this was long gone.

I suited up—mask, fins, the usual—and dove.

The water was cool and dim, the wreck looming out of the gloom like a fallen cathedral. I swam along the starboard hull first, noting the registration number still visible on the bow: *Aurora*, out of some port that no longer mattered. The cabin windows were smashed, the interior dark. I didn't go inside. I was here for machinery, not ghosts.

At the stern, I found the first motor. It was still mounted to the transom, an enormous electric outboard—50 kW, according to the faded label on the cowling—its propeller missing but its casing intact. The mounting bolts had held. The motor had been submerged for what looked like weeks, but the seals were still good; the casing showed no signs of water ingress. This was a prize beyond anything I'd salvaged before.

I surfaced, caught my breath, and swam around to the port side.

The second motor was still there, but only barely. Something had hit the port hull with a force that had sheared the transom in half, and the motor had taken a direct blow. The casing was split open along a diagonal line, the windings exposed and shredded, the rotor visibly cracked. It looked like a giant claw had swung through the boat and caught the motor on the way out. The cut was clean at the edges, the metal folded inward with the kind of pressure that came from something unimaginably strong.

I hung in the water for a moment, staring at the damage. A claw. A single, massive claw, large enough to slice through a fifty-foot catamaran and the high-torque electric motor attached to its stern. The monster that had done this was bigger than anything I'd seen in the chat reports. Bigger than the tentacles, bigger than the lobster swarms.

Anne's warning echoed in my head: *The ocean gets harder the longer you stay. Things change. The fog learns. The monsters get smarter.*

This wasn't a four-day attack. This was something else—something that had hunted a moving vessel and destroyed it with surgical precision.

I swallowed my fear and got to work.

The intact motor was the priority. I rigged the flotation bags—three of them, positioned to lift the motor evenly—and inflated them from the pressure bottle. The motor rose slowly, a sleek white torpedo suspended in silver balloons, and I guided it to the surface with careful hands. The crane hauled it aboard without complaint, its new reinforced mounting plate handling the weight as if it were nothing.

The broken motor was trickier. The split casing made it impossible to lift in one piece; I had to rig separate straps for the upper and lower sections, and even then, the flotation bags strained at their seams. The crane groaned when I winched it up, the arm flexing slightly before the load cleared the water. I set it down on the deck with a heavy clang, seawater streaming from its shattered interior.

I stood over the two motors, dripping and breathing hard, and assessed what I had.

The intact motor: 50 kW continuous, 80 kW peak, water-cooled, permanent magnet synchronous. A beautiful piece of engineering, built for a performance catamaran. It was larger than my current 10 kW unit, but the *Daydream*'s expanded hull could handle the weight, and the new battery bank could feed it without breaking a sweat.

The broken motor: a twin of the first, but gutted. The casing was salvageable, as were the bearings and some of the stator windings, but the rotor was scrap and the control electronics were a fused mess.

I sat on the deck next to them, Ember watching from the safety of the command station doorway, her ears pricked forward in feline curiosity.

And then the idea came.

The upgrade interface didn't just repair things. It disassembled and reassembled them at the molecular level. If I fed it one intact motor and the remains of a second, it could do more than fix the broken one. It could rebuild them both—redistribute the materials, rewind the stators, forge new rotors—and create two functional motors from the raw mass.

I sketched the design in my head: a pair of 45 kW motors, slightly detuned from the original to account for the loss of material, but still immensely powerful. Water-cooled, sealed to IP68, with integrated controllers that would plug directly into the *Daydream*'s electrical system. One motor for the boat. One motor as a spare, or for a future project, or for trade.

The interface accepted the design. The resource cost was surprisingly low—most of the mass was already there, and the detail I'd provided brought the fabrication cost down to almost nothing. I needed a small amount of copper for the rewinding and some electronic scrap for the new controllers, but my stocks were deep.

**Required Resources:**
- Copper: 1.2 kg
- Electronic Scrap: 1.5 units
- Aluminium (housings): 0.8 kg

I had it all.

I activated the fabrication. The two motors dissolved into a single, swirling cloud of metal and silicon, the broken casing and the intact windings merging into a silver-grey vortex that pulsed with its own internal light. The cloud divided, spun, and coalesced into two identical shapes. When the light faded, two pristine 45 kW motors sat on the deck, their casings a perfect matte white, their propeller shafts capped with protective covers, their control boxes integrated seamlessly into the housings.

They were identical. They were flawless. And they were mine.

I ran a diagnostic through the interface. Both motors passed every test. Continuous rating: 45 kW. Peak: 70 kW. Efficiency: 94% at cruise. They would bolt onto the *Daydream*'s transom with only minor modifications to the mounting bracket, and the existing throttle controls would interface directly with the new controllers. The upgrade was almost plug-and-play.

But I couldn't install one yet. The motor was only half the system. I needed a propeller to match—something larger, with a different pitch, optimized for the higher power and the *Daydream*'s new hull form. And I needed a new propshaft, thicker and stronger, to handle the torque.

Tomorrow, I would dive the wreck again and salvage what I could of the *Aurora*'s propulsion system. The catamaran had twin shafts and propellers; one might still be intact. If not, I had enough bronze and stainless steel in my stockpile to fabricate a new propeller from scratch. The interface could build anything I could design.

Tonight, I would disable the *Daydream*. I would unbolt the old 10 kW motor, disconnect its wiring, and lift it onto the deck. It would leave the boat dead in the water until the new motor was mounted—a day of vulnerability, maybe two. But I was in a safe area, the fog was far away, and the monsters were not due for another three days if I moved tomorrow. The risk was calculated.

I spent the evening on the preparations. I rigged the crane to lift the old motor from its well, labeling every wire and hose as I disconnected them. Ember supervised from her parts tray, occasionally meowing instructions I couldn't interpret. By sunset, the motor was on deck, a small, neat unit that had served me perfectly for eleven days and was about to become either a backup or a trade item. The *Daydream* sat silent, her propeller shaft a bare metal stub sticking out of the hull, her engine well empty.

I felt a pang of something—not fear, but vulnerability. The boat that had carried me through the first days was now crippled, dependent on my ability to finish what I'd started. But that was the point. I was not drifting. I was upgrading.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

*Day 11. 810 souls. Discovered wreck of catamaran "Aurora," destroyed by unknown large predator—claw marks suggest a monster significantly larger than any previously documented. Salvaged two 50 kW electric motors, one intact, one nearly severed. Used both as feedstock to fabricate two new 45 kW motors with integrated controllers. One will replace the old 10 kW unit; the second will serve as backup or trade.*

*Old motor removed. Boat currently immobile. Tomorrow: dive wreck for propshaft and propeller, or fabricate replacements. Complete installation by evening. Risk is minimal—fog distant, monster window open for another 72 hours. Ember is unimpressed by the chaos. She is a good anchor.*

*The claw marks bother me. That monster was not responding to stillness. It was hunting. Something to discuss with Anne, if she's willing.*

I sat on the aft porch for a while, Ember curled in my lap, watching the stars. The boat was still, her motor silent for the first time since Day 1. The quiet was unnerving, but also peaceful. Tomorrow, she would roar back to life with more power than I'd ever need.

And I would be ready for whatever the Expanse threw at me next.



























### Day 12

The *Daydream* lay still on the water, her hull rising and falling with the gentle swell, her systems alive but her heart missing. No hum from the motor well. No wake trailing behind. The solar panels were already drinking in the morning sun, the batteries full and waiting, but without a propeller to turn, all that power had nowhere to go.

Ember didn't mind. She was curled on my pillow, a warm comma of orange fur, purring in her sleep. I envied her that. She had no concept of vulnerability, no awareness that the boat was crippled. To her, the *Daydream* was a warm, fish-producing universe, and it had never occurred to her that it could fail.

I made coffee and drank it standing at the rail, staring at the water where the *Aurora* lay. The wreck was a shadow beneath the surface, its shattered bridgedeck visible in the morning light. Somewhere down there was the rest of my propulsion system. Time to find it.

Before I surfaced for the last time, I swam over the wreck's main deck and found an unexpected bonus: two canvas bags, still sealed in a deck locker, their waterproofing intact. I hauled them up and opened them on the aft porch. Sails. The *Aurora* had been a power catamaran, but someone had packed backup sails—a mainsail and a jib, heavy white Dacron, lightly used, with all their hardware. A prudent owner, probably thinking of ocean crossings and engine failures. They must have died before they could use them.

I didn't need sails. But someone, somewhere, needed them badly.

I spent the afternoon in the workshop.

The bronze bar was more than enough for two propellers. I measured the salvaged propeller carefully—blade pitch, diameter, hub dimensions, the curve of the leading edge—and transcribed everything into the upgrade interface. Then I modified the design: a matched pair, one left-handed and one right-handed, optimized for the *Daydream*'s hull form and the new 45 kW motor's torque curve. The interface calculated the material requirements, drew from the bronze bar and a small amount of stainless steel from my stockpile, and fabricated both propellers in a single shimmering cloud.

They were beautiful. Three-blade, perfectly balanced, their bronze gleaming like new gold. I mounted one on the spare shaft I'd cut down, fitted the shaft seals, and bolted the assembly to the new motor's coupling. The fit was precise, the tolerances tight, the alignment within a fraction of a degree.

By late afternoon, the 45 kW motor was in the well, the propeller was spinning freely, and the wiring was connected. I ran a diagnostic. Everything green.

I pressed the start button.

The motor came to life with a hum that was deeper than the old one—a smooth, powerful thrum that vibrated through the hull like a heartbeat. I pushed the throttle forward gently. The *Daydream* began to move, her propeller biting into the water, her bow swinging toward the open sea. 2 knots. 3 knots. 4 knots. The power draw was barely 3.5 kW at cruising speed—more efficient than the old motor, despite the increased power capacity, because the propeller was better matched to the hull.

I pushed the throttle further. 8 knots. 10 knots. The boat surged forward, her bow lifting, her wake flattening into a long, clean channel. The motor wasn't even warm. At 15 knots, the *Daydream* planed effortlessly, the world blurring past in a rush of wind and spray. I held it for a full minute, laughing into the salt air, before throttling back to a gentle 5 knots.

The sleeper speedboat was awake.

Ember emerged from the command station, her fur slightly ruffled by the wind, and meowed at me with what I chose to interpret as approval. She jumped onto the command sofa, curled into a tight ball, and went back to sleep. She was a cat. Speed was irrelevant. Comfort was eternal.

I set the autopilot to a slow, lazy course around the plateau—moving again, finally, the boat alive under my feet—and sat on the aft porch with the second 45 kW motor beside me. It was identical to the one in the well, pristine and powerful and ready for whatever came next. A spare. A trade item. A foundation for a second boat. The possibilities were still forming, but the motor was there, waiting.

The sun set in a blaze of copper and rose. I made dinner—rice and grilled fish, with a pinch of the last of my dried herbs—and sat on the porch, eating with Ember on my lap. The sea was calm, the stars emerging one by one, the motor humming its quiet song beneath the deck.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

Fabricated matched pair of bronze propellers from traded bar stock. Motor installed and tested: 45 kW continuous, 70 kW peak. Cruise efficiency improved—3.5 kW at 5 knots. Top speed tested at 15 knots; theoretical maximum likely higher. The sleeper speedboat is real.*

*Tomorrow: test endurance at higher speeds, map more of the plateau, and consider the next major project. The boat is becoming something beyond my original dream. I am becoming something beyond the man who woke here twelve days ago.*

I carried Ember to bed and lay in the dark, listening to the new motor's deeper hum, feeling the boat move beneath me with a power and grace that the old *Daydream* had only hinted at. The ocean was vast and dark and full of things that wanted to kill me. But I was faster now. Stronger. More capable.

And somewhere out there, Anne was watching, and the fog was learning, and the monsters were growing smarter. The long game had just begun.



























### Day 13

The morning came with a strange, flat light—high overcast that turned the sea to a sheet of hammered pewter. Ember, tucked into the crook of my arm, opened her eyes briefly, yawned, and closed them again. She had decided, over the past two days, that my pillow was now also her pillow, and I had not bothered to argue.

I lay still for a moment, listening to the new motor's deep, steady hum through the hull. It was a sound I had grown used to in less than twenty-four hours—lower than the old 10 kW unit, more felt than heard, a vibration in the bones rather than the ears. The *Daydream* was alive in a new way, and I had not yet fully tested her limits.

Today, I would.

I made coffee, fed Ember her usual slivers of fish, and stood on the aft porch studying the spare motor. It was still there, pristine in its matte white casing, identical to the one now pushing us through the water at a gentle 4 knots. I had fabricated two motors for a reason, and that reason was not just redundancy. The *Daydream*'s hull was wide enough now—4.5 meters across the beam—to handle twin screws, at least temporarily. If I wanted to know what this boat could truly do, I needed to see her under full power.

The decision took less time than the coffee.

I opened the upgrade interface and designed a temporary motor bracket: aluminium, heavily braced, that would bolt to the swim platform's reinforced edge and hold the second motor alongside the first. I didn't need a second helm or independent controls—just a simple Y-adapter for the throttle signal and a parallel power feed from the battery bus. The motors were identical, the controllers were identical, and the system would balance the load between them automatically.

**Required Resources:**
- Aluminium: 3.2 kg
- Stainless Steel Fasteners: 0.4 kg
- Copper Bus Bar: 0.6 kg

The cloud rose from my dwindling scrap pile and the bracket materialized on the deck, still warm from fabrication. It took an hour to bolt the second motor in place, connect the wiring, and mount the spare propeller—the right-handed one, matching the left-handed unit already in the well. The swim platform was crowded now, the two motors flanking the centerline like outboard twins on a much smaller boat. It wasn't elegant. It was, temporarily, functional.

I ran a diagnostic. Both motors showed green.

Ember watched from the command station doorway, her tail flicking with what might have been concern. I picked her up and set her in her nest of towels near the heater vent, where the worst of the acceleration wouldn't throw her around. She meowed once, then settled into a tight, watchful ball.

"All right," I said to the empty boat. "Let's see what you've got."

I pushed the throttle forward.

The *Daydream* surged like a thing possessed.

The motors sang together—a rising, synchronized whine that was louder than anything the boat had produced before, but still far quieter than any combustion engine. The bow lifted. The wake flattened into a long, white scar across the grey water. 10 knots. 15 knots. 20 knots. I watched the power readout climb: 12 kW on each motor, then 15, then 18. The boat planed effortlessly, her widened hull skimming the surface with a lightness that felt almost airborne.

At 20 kW per motor—40 kW total—the speed stabilized. 26 knots.

I held it there, the wind tearing at my hair, the sea blurring past in streaks of silver and grey. The motors were barely breathing; they were rated for 45 kW continuous each, and I was pulling less than half that. But the speed would not increase. I nudged the throttles higher, watching the power draw spike to 22 kW, 24 kW per motor, and the speed needle refused to budge. The water behind the boat churned white, a roiling, air-filled chaos that told me everything I needed to know.

Cavitation. The propellers were spinning faster than the water could flow into them, creating vacuum bubbles that collapsed with violent, pointless energy. The hull, too, was fighting me—17 meters was long, but it was not long enough to reach the kind of hydrodynamic efficiency that 26 knots demanded. I was pushing a displacement hull into planing speeds, and the physics were pushing back.

I throttled down, letting the boat settle back to a gentle 8 knots, and sat at the helm breathing hard from a tension I hadn't realized I'd been holding.

26 knots. On a solar-powered liveaboard built from dreams and salvage. It was absurd. It was magnificent. And it was not fast enough—not because I needed more speed, but because I knew, with an engineer's bone-deep certainty, that the boat was capable of more if I fixed the fundamentals. A longer hull. Larger propellers. A drivetrain that matched the power I already had.

But that was a project for the coming week. Right now, another thought was gnawing at me.

The *Aurora*.

I had been pushing the thought aside since I'd found the wreck, letting it sit in the back of my mind while I focused on salvage and fabrication. But now, with the boat humming beneath me and the empty sea stretching in every direction, the pieces began to fall into place.

The *Aurora* had been a moving vessel. Not anchored, not drifting—a fast catamaran under power, probably making good speed. And something had hit it anyway. Something with a claw large enough to shear through a fifty-foot hull and the high-torque motor attached to its stern. That was not a four-day attack. That was something else.

The alien ship girl—I still didn't know her name—had shared her discovery about territorial monsters. Different areas had different species. They didn't overlap. They had ranges, boundaries, hunting grounds. The four-day timer applied to stationary vessels within those territories. But what if there was a second timer? A longer one, ticking away regardless of movement, triggered by presence rather than stillness.

What if you could stay in a monster's territory too long, even while moving, and it would eventually come for you regardless?

Anne had hinted at it, though I couldn't tell the girl that. *The ocean gets harder the longer you stay. Things change. The fog learns. The monsters get smarter.* She had been here 410 days, and she was still alive. She knew something about long-term survival that I didn't. And the *Aurora*—had it been circling the same waters for weeks, unaware that it was being watched, tracked, marked?

A longer hull—22 meters, maybe 24—would stretch the waterline and raise the hull speed without needing to plane. Larger propellers, with more blade area and a shallower pitch, would bite cleaner water and resist cavitation at higher power settings. And a single, larger motor—maybe a custom 80 kW unit built from the two 45s I already had—would let me eliminate the drag of the temporary twin-motor bracket.

The design was taking shape in my notebook. I sketched a longer stern, a refined swim platform, a propeller tunnel to let the blades run in cleaner water. The resource list was daunting—more steel, more fibreglass, more copper—but my stockpile was growing, and my trading reputation was solid. I could source what I needed.

As the sun set, I stood on the aft porch with Ember on my shoulder and watched the grey water fade to black. The boat was moving fast, the wake a long, straight line pointing south toward the plateau I was leaving behind. Somewhere beneath that water, if my theory was right, something was watching. Waiting. Its internal clock ticking down.

But I would not be there when the alarm rang.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

### Day 15

The insulation foam would fill the hull's cavities, providing buoyancy and thermal protection. Then another two days of work at a new location, expanding further, integrating the foils, installing the outboard pod. Then another travel day, and a final two days of work to finish the superstructure, the interior, the fine details.

Two days on, one day off, repeated over eight days. By the end, the *Daydream* would be unrecognizable—a 42-meter hydrofoil yacht that could cruise at 10 knots on a whisper of power and sprint at over 40 knots when the foils deployed.

By the end, I would have built my dream in truth.

### Day 16

The chat was quiet—the ferry flotilla had apparently found an island and was setting up camp, their messages full of cautious optimism. I didn't trust it. The territorial timer theory nagged at me. If they stayed in one place too long, the watchers would find them, and no amount of community spirit would save them.

But I had my own survival to worry about.

The next stage of the transformation required careful planning. Tomorrow, I would tow the barge north. The *Daydream*'s current hull could handle the load—the 45 kW motor had torque to spare—but I needed to rig a proper towing bridle and reinforce the stern cleats. I spent an hour designing and fabricating a heavy-duty towing assembly: a bridle of steel cable running from both stern corners to a central tow point, with a quick-release mechanism in case of emergency. The barge's bow had a massive towing bitt, and I rigged a fifty-meter tow line from it to the bridle, with chafing gear at every contact point.

I tested the setup briefly, engaging the motor at low power and feeling the *Daydream* take the strain. The tow line went taut. The barge began to move. I killed the motor after a minute—no need to burn power on a test—but the proof was there. I could tow the barge. Slowly, probably no more than 3 or 4 knots, but I could move it.

---

The evening was for design.

I sat at the nav desk with Ember on my lap, the upgrade interface open, the simulation mode rendering the final transformation in rotating 3D. The 42-meter hull was breathtaking—a long, slender arrow of a boat, her bow raked back at a sharp angle, her stern wide and flat to accommodate the hydrofoil pod. The foils themselves were works of art: swept-back wings with a span of 8 meters when deployed, their cross-sections optimized for lift at speed. The hydraulic cylinders that would raise and lower them were integrated into the hull structure, protected from the sea by fairings.

The interior was taking shape too. The master cabin would move amidships, where the motion was gentlest, with a proper bed and storage and even a small desk. The command station would be on the upper deck, a true pilothouse with 360-degree visibility. The galley would be a proper kitchen, with counter space and a dining table. The workbench would become a workshop, with room for the 3D printer and the tools and the spare parts I was accumulating. And there would be space for Ember—a cat tree, a scratching post, a sunny spot by the window.

I was not building a survival boat anymore. I was building a home.

The interface calculated the resource requirements for Stage 2: the full hull extension, the foil mounting points, the propulsion pod integration. It would consume 45 tons of material—most of the aluminium and half the timber. Stage 3 would add the foils themselves, the hydraulic system, and the pod's reduction gear. Stage 4 would finish the superstructure, the interior, and the fine details.

I saved the design and sat back, my eyes tired but my mind racing. The *Daydream* was going to be magnificent.

---

Before bed, I made a final round of the deck, checking the towing bridle, the cargo lashings, the solar panel mounts. Everything was secure. The barge sat quietly in the darkness, its anchor light a small LED I'd rigged from a salvaged lantern. The sea was calm, the stars bright, the air cool and clean.

Ember met me at the door, meowing her evening demand for attention. I picked her up and carried her to the temporary berth near the helm—the master cabin was still buried under supplies—and lay down with her on my chest.

"Tomorrow we move," I told her. "Slowly, probably. But by the end of the week, we'll have a ship that can fly."

She purred. She didn't care about foils or solar panels or towing bridles. But she cared about the warmth of my chest and the rhythm of my heartbeat, and for now, that was enough for both of us.

I wrote in my logbook before sleep:

*Day 16. 801 souls. Solar array expanded significantly through trade—22 kW peak now installed, sufficient for towing and final configuration. Caught 80 lb yellowfin tuna; traded portions for panels and goodwill. Transferred additional 30 tons of material from barge to Daydream. Total aboard now 70 tons. Towing bridle fabricated and tested. Barge ready for departure tomorrow.*

*Stage 2-4 designs finalized: 42m LOA, 7.8m beam, hydrofoil-assisted, 90 kW propulsion pod. Interior plans include proper workshop, galley, master cabin amidships, and a cat tree for Ember. Transformation will consume approximately 95 tons of the 125 tons available, with remainder for future upgrades.*

*Tomorrow: begin towing barge north. Travel day. Use the time to refine foil geometry and hydraulic system design. Ember is adjusting well to the chaos—she has claimed the nav desk chair as her secondary throne.*

*The Expanse is still out there. The fog. The monsters. The ferry's doomed flotilla. But I am building something they cannot touch. Something that will outrun anything the ocean throws at us.*

I closed my eyes. The motor hummed. The boat rocked. And somewhere in the dark, a barge full of dreams waited for the dawn.












### Day 17

The fog came from the south like a slow, dark thought.

I saw it first on the chart plotter, a red smear bleeding across the map's lower edge, and for a moment I refused to believe it. I had been tracking fog movements for over two weeks. I knew their patterns—their lazy drifts, their currents, their tendency to follow deep-water channels. This bank should not have been here. It should not have been anywhere near my position. But the radar painted it clear and cold, a wall of something that wasn't water and wasn't air, crawling toward me at a steady pace.

I stood at the helm, one hand on the throttle, the other holding a mug of coffee that had gone cold twenty minutes ago. The barge was a heavy presence behind me, its tow line creaking with each swell. The solar panels were drinking in the morning sun, pushing 15 kilowatts into the system, and I was using every single watt of it to pull the barge north at a miserable 4 knots. The batteries were full. I had been keeping them full, hoarding their charge like a miser, because I knew that towing at night would drain them fast.

Now I might need that charge sooner than I'd planned.

I checked the numbers again. The fog was twenty kilometers away and moving at 4 and a half knots. If it kept that speed, it would reach me in about 21 hours. And I was making 4 knots while towing the barge.

The math was brutal. I could outrun it if I cut the barge loose—the *Daydream* could sprint at 18 knots now, and even cruising at 9 knots would leave the fog far behind, with a better motor it could even reach 26 knots. But cutting the barge meant losing seventy tons of irreplaceable material. The wood, the aluminium, the insulation foam. The hydrofoil yacht I'd been designing. All of it, gone.

I looked at the barge through the aft window. It rode low and heavy, its deck still piled with timber bundles and foam pallets, all of it lashed down and ready for the next stage of the transformation. I had poured two days of labor into that barge. I had built the *Daydream*'s future from its bones.

But I had also made a promise, unspoken but absolute, to the small orange kitten currently asleep on the command sofa. Ember had no concept of fog or hydrofoils or sunk cost. She only knew that I was warm and that fish appeared when she meowed. She trusted me to keep her safe, and that trust was the most precious thing I had left from the old world.

I couldn't gamble her life on a math problem.

I set my coffee down and began to prepare.

---

The safe box was something I'd designed three days ago, late at night while Ember slept on my lap and I thought about worst-case scenarios. It was a simple thing—a compartment built into the space beneath the command station, originally a storage locker for charts and electronics. I had reinforced it with aluminium plate, lined it with closed-cell foam for impact absorption, and fitted a mesh net across the opening that could be secured from inside or out. The interior was 1.5 meters long, 0.7 meters wide, and 0.7 meters high—large enough for a cat carrier, a small water bowl, and a towel that smelled like me.

I had hoped I would never need to use it.

I lifted Ember from the sofa, cradling her against my chest. She blinked up at me with sleepy blue eyes, her purr stuttering in confusion. She was barely three weeks old, a scrap of orange fur and needle claws, and she weighed less than a bag of flour. Her whole world was the boat.

"Hey, little one," I said, carrying her to the safe box. "We have a situation. I need you to be brave for a while."

She meowed—a small, questioning sound. I tucked her into the box, on the towel, with a small bowl of water and a piece of dried fish. She sniffed the towel, circled twice, and sat down. She didn't look scared. She looked mildly annoyed.

I secured the mesh net across the opening. "I'll be right here. I'm not leaving. But if things get rough, you'll be safe in there. Okay?"

She meowed again. I chose to interpret it as understanding.

The box was positioned so I could see it from the helm, and so Ember could see me. If the fog caught us—if something went wrong—she would not be thrown around the cabin. She would not be in the way. She would be as safe as I could make her, and that thought gave me a grim, steadying comfort.

I straightened up and checked the chart plotter. The fog was eighteen kilometers away and still closing. Two hours had passed since I'd first spotted it. The speed differential was unchanged.

I went aft to the tow bridle and stood looking at the barge.

Seventy tons of material. Enough to build a ship that could fly. It was a fortune, and it was dragging me toward death at half a knot's difference. I could save it if I ran the motor harder, drew from the batteries, pushed the speed up to 6 or 7 knots and hoped the fog didn't accelerate further. But that would drain the batteries, and if the fog kept coming through the night, I would have nothing left by dawn.

Or I could cut it loose.

The axe was in my hand before I remembered picking it up. The quick-release mechanism I'd designed was fail-safe—a single lever that would detach the tow line from the bridle, clean and instant. I could pull it now, throttle up, and be twenty miles away before sunset. The barge would drift. The fog would take it, maybe. Or it wouldn't. It would still be there tomorrow, if the fog missed it, and I could come back.

But I knew, standing there with the axe, that I wouldn't come back. If I cut the barge loose, I would keep going north, and I would never see those timber bundles again. The hydrofoil would remain a design in the interface, a dream that never became real.

I looked at the barge. I looked at the fog. I looked at Ember, a tiny orange shadow behind the mesh net, watching me with her unblinking blue eyes.

I was not a reckless man. I had survived this long by being cold and careful and utterly unsentimental. But the barge was more than materials. It was the culmination of everything I'd learned—about the interface, about the Expanse, about myself. Cutting it loose felt like cutting away a part of who I was becoming.

I tightened my grip on the axe and began to calculate one last time.

---

The afternoon was a slow, tense eternity.

I pushed the motor to 20 kilowatts, pulling from the batteries now, raising our speed to 5 knots. The barge creaked behind us, the tow line humming with strain. The solar panels were still feeding 15 kilowatts into the system.

And the fog kept coming.

By 2 PM it was twelve kilometers away. By 4 PM, ten. I stood at the helm, watching the distance close on the chart plotter, feeling the cold prickle of dread crawl up my spine. The fog bank was visible now on the horizon—a low, black wall that ate the light. It didn't look like weather. It looked like something alive, something patient, something that had decided I was worth the effort.

I talked to Ember through the mesh. Stupid things, mostly. Descriptions of what I was doing, reassurances that I didn't entirely believe. She watched me with her steady blue gaze and didn't meow. She knew, in the way that animals know, that something was wrong.

By 6 PM, the fog was eight kilometers away and I had made a decision. I would give it one more hour. If the distance hadn't stabilized by then, I would cut the barge loose. The materials weren't worth her life. They weren't worth mine.

I went aft and checked the tow line, the bridle, the quick-release lever. Everything was in order. The axe was sharp. The motor was warm but running clean. The batteries were still at 91 percent—I'd been careful, keeping the draw low, letting the solar do the heavy work.

I stood on the aft deck, one hand on the release lever, and watched the fog.

And then, impossibly, it began to slow.

The change was subtle at first—a fractional drop in the closing speed on the chart plotter. I feared it was a sensor error, a glitch in the radar's tracking algorithm. But the numbers kept shifting. The fog's speed dropped to 3, then knots to 2.5, then to 2. The distance between us stabilized for a second and then increased.

I stood there for a full fifteen minutes, not daring to move, watching the numbers. The fog was still moving. It was still coming. But it was no longer gaining. The gap increased as the sun dipped toward the horizon, the black wall a grim companion on the southern horizon, slowly retreating.

I let out a breath I hadn't realized I'd been holding. My hands were shaking. I hadn't noticed that either.

I went inside, opened the mesh net, and scooped Ember into my arms. She purred immediately, butting her head against my chin, and I held her close, feeling her tiny heartbeat against my palm.

"We're not out of this yet," I told her. "But we're not cutting the barge loose. Not tonight."

She purred louder.

---

The night was long.

I didn't sleep. I couldn't. The fog was still there, now twelve kilometers back, its speed now matched to mine with an eerie precision that suggested intelligence rather than coincidence. Every time I adjusted the throttle, the fog adjusted its pace. Not enough to close the gap, but enough to maintain it. It was pacing me.

I kept the motor at 8 kilowatts, making a steady 2 knots. The solar panels were dead for the night, their output zero, and the batteries were carrying the load alone. I watched the charge level tick down—90 percent, 80, 70—with a mounting anxiety that I couldn't afford to indulge. I needed to keep moving. If I stopped, the fog would be on us in a little over three hours.

Ember slept in the safe box, her small body rising and falling with each breath. I checked on her every fifteen minutes, a ritual that was as much for my comfort as hers. She was fine. She was always fine. She was a cat, and cats are built to endure, but she was also a tiny, fragile thing that depended on me completely, and the weight of that responsibility was heavier than the barge I was towing.

At midnight, the fog began to fall back.

The chart plotter showed its speed dropping to 1.5 knots, then 1 knot. The gap widened to fourteen kilometers, then sixteen. By 3 AM, the fog was a faint red smear on the southern edge of the radar, its threat receding with every mile I put between us. I didn't know why it had given up. Maybe it had reached the limit of its territory. Maybe the currents had shifted. Maybe it had simply decided I wasn't worth the effort.

I didn't care. I just kept moving.

At dawn, the fog was gone.

The sun rose on an empty sea, the water a pale, placid blue, the sky clear from horizon to horizon. The radar showed nothing. The chart plotter showed nothing. The only evidence that the fog had ever been there was the knot of cold dread still coiled in my stomach and the battery readout, which now showed 17 percent.

17 percent. I had run the motor all night, and the battery-bank had held. Barely.

I throttled back to a gentle 3 knots and let the solar panels begin their work. The morning sun pushed 8 kilowatts into the system, and the battery readout ticked upward—21 percent, 22 percent. By noon, it would be back above 50. By evening, full.

I released the helm, staggered to the safe box, and lifted Ember into my arms. She stretched, yawned, and licked my chin with her tiny sandpaper tongue.

"We made it," I said, my voice hoarse. "We made it, little one."

She purred.

I sat on the command sofa with her on my chest and let the exhaustion wash over me. The barge was still there, its tow line creaking, its deck piled with the materials that would transform the *Daydream* into a flying ship. The fog was gone. Ember was safe. I was still alive.

I closed my eyes for just a moment, and when I opened them again, the sun was high and the batteries were at 45 percent and the world was still turning.

Before I let myself sleep properly, I checked the chat—a quick scroll, nothing more. The flotilla had found a promising island, a green smudge on someone's sonar, and they were setting up a base camp while the ferry continued to pick up stragglers. The battleship was still patrolling. The alien ship girl was still mapping. The world went on.

I wrote in my logbook, the words slow and deliberate:

*Day 17. 799 souls. Fog pursued us for 18 hours, closing to 7 km before falling back. Behavior anomalous—speed up to 3 knots, pacing, possible intelligence. Did not cut barge. Ember secured in safe box throughout. Batteries drained to 17 percent overnight; solar now recharging. Both alive. Both safe. The fog is learning. But so am I.*

*Tomorrow: resume Stage 2 transformation. The hydrofoil will fly. Nothing will stop that.*

I set the autopilot, fed Ember her breakfast, and finally, finally, let myself sleep.









### Day 18

I woke to the smell of coffee and the distant cry of a seabird.

For a long, disoriented moment, I couldn't place myself. The light was wrong—too bright, too high—and the boat felt different beneath me, heavier and more solid. Then memory flooded back: the fog, the long night, the battery readout ticking down to twenty percent. I had fallen asleep on the command sofa just after dawn, Ember still clutched to my chest, the solar panels already beginning their slow, patient work.

I sat up. Every muscle in my body protested. My eyes felt like sandpaper. My head was full of cotton. I had slept for perhaps three hours, and the exhaustion was still there, a low, grinding weight beneath the adrenaline that had carried me through the night.

Ember was gone from my chest. I found her in the safe box—I'd left the mesh open—curled on her towel, watching me with the serene patience of a creature who had slept perfectly well and was now waiting for breakfast. The water bowl was empty. The dried fish was gone.

"Right," I said, my voice a croak. "Food. Coffee. Then work."

I checked the systems while the coffee brewed. Batteries at 68 percent and climbing, the solar array already pushing 14 kilowatts into the bank. The barge was still there, its tow line creaking gently in the morning swell. The sea was calm, the sky clear, the horizon empty in every direction. The fog had not returned.

I fed Ember, made myself a double portion of oatmeal, and drank two cups of coffee while standing at the helm. The exhaustion was not going away, but I had learned, in the old world, how to function on no sleep. Caffeine and determination. It wasn't healthy, but it was necessary.

I had a boat to build.

---

The barge had to go.

Not just be towed—consumed. Every scrap of aluminium, every splinter of wood, every sheet of fibreglass and block of foam. The hull extension I had designed required almost all of it, and I couldn't fabricate the new hull while the materials were still sitting on a separate vessel a hundred feet behind me. Everything had to be aboard the *Daydream* before I could begin.

I started with the timber bundles. The crane swung them over one by one, the hydraulic winch whining under the strain, the bundles settling onto the *Daydream*'s deck with deep, resonant thuds. Each bundle weighed four tons; I stacked them along the centerline, securing them with ratchet straps, building a wall of wood that rose higher than the cabin roof. Ember watched from the command station window, her ears pricked, her tail flicking with each thud.

By late morning, the timber was aboard. Thirty bundles, eighty tons of marine plywood and dimensional lumber, enough to frame a ship three times the *Daydream*'s current size. The deck was crowded, the gunwales riding low, but the temporary cargo hull I'd built on Day 15 was handling the load.

The insulation foam came next. Ten pallets of closed-cell boards, lighter than the timber but bulkier. I stacked them on top of the wood, lashing everything down with cargo nets. The crane worked flawlessly—I'd built it well, and the reinforced mounting plate showed no signs of strain.

Then the aluminium deck plates. I cut them free with the circular saw, the blade screaming through the metal, sparks showering into the sea. Each plate was eight feet by twelve, heavy and awkward, and I had to rig a spreader bar to lift them without bending. It was slow work, methodical and exhausting, and by early afternoon my arms were trembling and my shirt was soaked with sweat.

I took a break at 2 PM, sitting on a pallet of foam with Ember on my lap, eating a cold piece of leftover tuna and drinking a full liter of water. The *Daydream* was a floating lumberyard now, her decks buried under a mountain of material, her waterline higher than I'd ever seen it. She looked like a cargo scow. She looked like chaos. But she was carrying everything I needed.

"One more push," I told Ember. "The aluminium struts, the fibreglass panels, and the steel fittings. Then we're done with the barge."

She meowed and butted her head against my chin. I took that as agreement.

The afternoon was a blur of physical labor. I cut the barge's aluminium frame apart with the saw, the sections clanging onto the deck. I stripped the fibreglass panels from the deckhouse remnants, their edges sharp and splintery. I salvaged every bolt, every bracket, every scrap of steel that I could unbolt or cut free. The crane never stopped moving, lifting and swinging and lowering, until the barge was a bare skeleton, its deck empty, its frame stripped to the ribs.

By 5 PM, there was nothing left on the barge but the hull itself—a hundred-meter aluminium tub that I had no use for and no way to lift. I cut the tow line and let it drift, an empty shell on the endless blue. It had served its purpose. Its bones would become my new ship.

I stood on the *Daydream*'s aft deck, breathing hard, sweat dripping into my eyes, and surveyed the mountain of material that surrounded me. Timber, foam, aluminium, fibreglass, steel. One hundred and ten tons of potential, all of it lashed down and waiting.

It was time.

---

I went inside and opened the upgrade interface. The design for Stage 2 was already loaded—I had refined it obsessively over the past two days, every frame and stringer and bulkhead specified in exact detail. The interface had never seen a design this complex, this ambitious. It pulsed softly, as if eager.

Forty-two meters long. Seven-point-eight meters wide. A hull shaped like a drawn bowstring, long and lean, with a wave-piercing bow that would slice through heavy seas rather than pound over them. The stern was wide and flat, reinforced to mount the propulsion pod and the central hydrofoil. The mounting points for the forward foils were integrated into the hull structure, massive aluminium brackets that would later hold the hydraulic rams. The deck was stepped, with a raised foredeck, a central cockpit, and a vast aft deck that would eventually become the workshop and storage area.

Inside, the transformation was equally radical. The command station would move to a raised pilothouse amidships, with proper visibility and a dedicated navigation desk. The master cabin would shift to the center of the boat, where the motion was gentlest, with a real bed and storage and a hatch to the deck. The galley would become a proper kitchen, with counter space and a dining table and a pantry. The bathroom would be a real bathroom, not a closet. The workshop would be a room of its own, with benches and tool racks and space for the 3D printer and the spare parts I was accumulating.

And Ember would have her cat tree by the window.

I checked the interface's material estimate one last time. The piles on deck matched the requirements within a few percent. I would have almost nothing left over, but the result would be a ship that could cross any ocean on a whisper of power.

I walked the deck one final time, checking the lashings, making sure the materials were all within the fabrication envelope. Ember followed me, a tiny orange shadow weaving between the timber stacks. When I stopped, she jumped onto my shoulder—her favorite perch—and curled her tail around my neck.

"Ready?" I asked her.

She purred.

I activated the fabrication.

---

The cloud that rose this time was not like the others.

It was vast—a nebula of silver and gold and brown and white that erupted from every pile of material simultaneously, blotting out the sun, turning the world into a swirling, luminous fog. The timber bundles unraveled into streams of golden motes. The aluminium plates dissolved into rivers of mercury light. The insulation foam became a soft, billowing mist that glowed with its own inner radiance. The fibreglass panels shimmered and came apart like threads of glass. The steel fittings disintegrated into specks of iron-blue fire.

The cloud rose and expanded, enveloping the *Daydream* completely. I stood at its center, Ember on my shoulder, and felt the boat begin to change.

The deck shifted beneath my feet. Not the violent shudder of an earthquake, but a slow, fluid motion, like the boat was breathing. The hull stretched forward and aft simultaneously, the bow pushing out into the sea, the stern extending behind us until the swim platform was a distant memory. The beam widened, the gunwales pushing outward, the deck spreading until I could no longer see both rails from the centerline. The cabin roof rose, the windows expanding, the interior spaces blooming outward like time-lapse footage of a building under construction.

The command station lifted and shifted, the helm sliding forward and upward into a new pilothouse that materialized around me. The windows formed from swirling glass motes, seamless and curved, wrapping around the helm in a panoramic arc. The nav desk appeared to my left, the sofa to my right, the chart plotter glowing on its new, larger console. The instruments arranged themselves with perfect logic, every switch and gauge exactly where I would have placed it.

Below, the master cabin formed around the space where I'd once kept storage lockers. The bed materialized—a proper queen-sized berth with storage beneath—and the bulkheads flowed into place, white and clean, with the same blue LED accent lighting. The galley expanded, counters stretching, cabinets multiplying, the induction hob now a four-burner unit. The bathroom grew a full shower stall. The workshop became a room of its own, with a door and a ventilation system and enough bench space to build an engine.

And Ember's cat tree—a simple thing of carpeted platforms and a dangling toy—materialized by the starboard pilothouse window, exactly where the morning sun would hit it.

The cloud began to settle. The light dimmed. The humming that had vibrated through my bones faded to silence.

I stood in the pilothouse of a 42-meter hydrofoil yacht, Ember still on my shoulder, and for a long moment I could not speak.

---

I walked the new boat in a daze.

The pilothouse was magnificent. The windows wrapped around me, giving a view of the sea that was almost 360 degrees. The helm console was clean and intuitive, every system status glowing green. The nav desk had room for my laptop, my logbook, and a cup of coffee with space to spare. The sofa was large enough to sleep on, upholstered in the same charcoal fabric I'd chosen for the original *Daydream*.

I descended the stairs—real stairs now, not a ladder—to the living deck. The galley was a kitchen that would not have been out of place in a small apartment. The dining table seated four. The bathroom had a separate shower compartment and a composting toilet that looked like it belonged in a luxury RV. The workshop was a proper workshop, with a heavy workbench, a vise, tool drawers, and a bulkhead covered in pegboard that was already populated with my tools, arranged by size and type.

The master cabin was forward of the living area, its door opening onto a space that was larger than the entire original *Daydream* cabin. The bed was made with white linens. The portholes were larger, letting in the golden light of the setting sun. A small desk sat beneath one window, and a hanging locker held my limited wardrobe. Ember's towel was already on the pillow, as if the interface had known.

And the deck space—I walked out onto the aft deck and simply stood there, turning slowly, trying to take it all in. The deck stretched for meters in every direction. The crane was still there, remounted on a reinforced base. The solar array had been reorganized, the panels arranged in a sweeping arch over the aft deck that doubled as a sunshade. The forward deck was wide and clear, the mounting points for the hydrofoils visible as polished aluminium sockets set into the hull. The stern was flat and massive, the space for the propulsion pod a sealed, reinforced well that would eventually hold the 90-kilowatt drive unit.

The boat was a skeleton, still. The superstructure was minimal—the pilothouse and the cabin roof were finished, but the decks were bare aluminium plate, and the interior walls were unpainted. The foils were not yet installed. The pod was not yet mounted. The fine details—the wood trim, the fabric panels, the lighting—were still to come.

But the hull was real. The space was real. The bones of my dream were there, solid and true, waiting to be filled in.

I walked back to the pilothouse and sat down heavily on the sofa. Ember jumped from my shoulder to her cat tree, sniffed the platforms with intense concentration, and then curled up on the top level, directly in the last patch of sunlight. She closed her eyes and began to purr.

I watched her for a moment, and then I started to laugh. A tired, giddy, disbelieving laugh that echoed through the empty spaces of my enormous new boat.

Three hundred and twenty-seven square meters of deck. A hull that could cross oceans. The foundation of a ship that could fly. And a kitten, purring in the sun, utterly unimpressed by any of it.

I had been awake for almost forty hours, with a three-hour nap in the middle. I had fought off a fog bank, stripped a barge to its bones, and transformed my 14-meter liveaboard into a 42-meter hydrofoil yacht. I was exhausted beyond words.

But I was home.

---

I didn't bother with a proper dinner. I ate a handful of dried fruit and a piece of cold fish, standing at the galley counter, too tired to sit. Ember got her evening portion, and I made sure her water bowl was full. The batteries were at 82 percent—the solar had done its work. The systems were all green. The boat was drifting gently on the evening swell, her new hull riding the water with a grace that the old *Daydream* had never known.

I carried Ember to the master cabin—her first time sleeping there—and set her on the pillow. She kneaded it briefly, circled three times, and curled into a ball. I lay down beside her, still in my salt-stained clothes, and pulled the duvet over us both.

Before sleep took me, I wrote in my logbook, the words sloppy and barely legible:

*Day 18. 797 souls. Stage 2 complete. Hull extended to 42m LOA, 7.8m beam. All barge materials consumed. Interior spaces framed, systems functional, but finishing still required. Foil mounting points, pod well, and structural reinforcements in place. The boat is unrecognizable. The boat is magnificent.*

*Ember has a cat tree. She approves. I haven't slept properly in two days. Tomorrow: rest, and then Stage 3. The foils. The pod. The wings that will let us fly.*

*Tonight, I sleep in a bed that feels like a continent. The sea is calm. The fog is gone. We are still moving. We are still alive.*

I closed the notebook, set it on the new desk, and let the darkness take me. Ember purred against my chest, a tiny engine of warmth, and the *Daydream*—my impossible, magnificent, ever-growing *Daydream*—rocked us gently into sleep.

















### Day 19

I woke to the unfamiliar sensation of space.

The master cabin stretched around me, its white bulkheads still bare but no longer cramped. Morning light poured through the larger portholes, painting golden rectangles on the duvet. Ember was not on the pillow. I found her a moment later, perched on the highest platform of her new cat tree, batting at a dangling toy with the focused intensity of a predator who had never known hunger. She had already eaten—I could tell from the small piece of fish skin still stuck to her whisker.

"Good morning to you too," I said.

She chirped, a short, bright sound that meant *I have conquered this tower and all its toys are mine*.

I stretched, feeling the ache in my muscles from yesterday's labor. Three hours of sleep after forty hours awake was not enough, but the long night's rest had restored me. I felt almost human again.

The boat rocked gently, her new hull responding to the swell with a slow, dignified roll. She was no longer the nimble 14-meter cutter that had dodged fog banks in the first days. She was something larger, something that moved through the water with the gravity of a small ship. But she was still mine.

After coffee and a proper breakfast—oatmeal with dried fruit, eaten at the new galley table—I sat down to plan the day's work. The interface showed the remaining stages of the transformation: propulsion pod integration, foil mounting points, hydraulic system, and the final superstructure details.

But first, a small detour.

I had found the coat while stripping the barge's deckhouse: a heavy wool peacoat, dark blue, still wrapped in plastic from some long-forgotten supply run. It was too large for me, and far too warm for this climate. But the fabric was good, and Ember had been trying to play with loose screws and cable ties all week. She needed something that wasn't a choking hazard.

I sat at the workbench and opened the upgrade interface. The design was simple—a small teddy bear, no taller than my hand, stuffed with scraps of insulation foam and weighted with a few steel beads for heft. The wool would become the outer fabric; I specified the seam placement, the ear shape, the embroidered eyes. The interface accepted the design with a resource cost so minimal it barely registered.

The wool dissolved into a soft grey cloud and reformed on the bench: a small, slightly lopsided teddy bear, its fur a deep navy blue, its eyes two tiny knots of black thread. It was not a masterpiece. It was not elegant. But it was soft and warm and exactly the right size for a kitten to wrestle.

I carried it to the pilothouse and set it on the floor. Ember spotted it immediately. She froze, her tail twitching, her entire being focused on the small blue intruder. Then she pounced.

I left her to her battle and went to work.

---

The propulsion pod was the heart of the new *Daydream*.

I had two 45-kilowatt motors, identical and pristine, waiting on the aft deck. Individually, they were powerful. Combined, they would be something else entirely—a single 90-kilowatt electric drive, water-cooled, sealed against the deep, mounted on a steerable pod that would hang beneath the stern like an outboard engine for a much smaller boat. The pod would give me vectored thrust, eliminating the need for a rudder, and its internal reduction gear would let me swing a propeller large enough to push the massive new hull at respectable speeds even without the foils.

I opened the upgrade interface and began the design. The two motors would be merged into a single unit, their rotors aligned on a common shaft, their stators rewound to share the magnetic field. The casing would be streamlined aluminium, shaped to minimize drag when the boat was foilborne—someday, when the foils could actually lift us. The reduction gear would be a simple planetary set, stepping the motor's high RPM down to the propeller's optimal range. The steering mechanism would be hydraulic, with a backup manual system in case of failure.

The interface hummed as I fed it the details. The two motors dissolved into a single, swirling cloud of silver and copper, and when it coalesced, the pod lay on the deck before me: a sleek, torpedo-shaped unit three meters long, its propeller a massive five-blade bronze wheel already mounted on the shaft. It was beautiful in the way that only well-engineered things are beautiful.

I spent the morning installing it. The pod well I'd built into the stern yesterday was perfectly sized—the interface had seen to that—and the mounting bolts slid into place with satisfying precision. I connected the power cables, the control wiring, the hydraulic lines for steering. The cooling system I plumbed into the hull's existing seawater loop, with a heat exchanger that would keep the motor at optimal temperature even under heavy load.

By noon, the pod was mounted and the tests were green. I ran a brief diagnostic at low power, feeling the propeller churn the water behind us, the boat surging forward with a smooth, silent power that made the old 10-kilowatt motor feel like a toy. At 30 kilowatts, the *Daydream* cruised at 9 knots, her long hull slipping through the water with barely a ripple. At 60 kilowatts, she hit 14 knots, her bow lifting slightly, her wake a clean, flat channel. At the full 90 kilowatts—just a brief burst—she surged past 18 knots, the speed climbing with a steadiness that suggested more was possible.

But only so much more. The hull was long and sleek, but without the foils, she was still a displacement vessel at heart. The physics were unforgiving. I ran the numbers in my head as I throttled back to a gentle cruise.

To lift 130 tons of aluminium and wood and dreams out of the water on hydrofoils, I would need something like 3 thousand kilowatts. Maybe 4 thousand. The current motor could generate 90. Even if I stripped the boat to bare hull and sacrificed every comfort, the gap was orders of magnitude too wide. The foils I was about to install would be useless without a power source that didn't exist yet.

I sat at the helm for a long moment, staring at the empty sea. I could switch to diesel—the interface could probably build a marine turbine or a bank of high-speed engines that would burn the fuel I'd traded away. But diesel was heavy, noisy, finite, and dependent on salvage that was already becoming scarce. It was a step backward, toward the desperate chaos of the first days, and I had not come this far to go backward.

The other option was batteries. A lot of batteries. Tens of thousands of kilowatt-hours of storage, enough to feed the foils for the brief sprints that would make them worthwhile. But the lithium cells I had were standard marine-grade units; to store that much energy, I would need a bank the size of a small house, and the weight would cancel out any benefit the foils could provide. I needed something denser. Something the old world had not yet invented.

The upgrade interface could, in theory, create such a thing. I had proven that the interface could build anything I could design in sufficient detail. But I did not know how to design a better battery. I knew the chemistry of lithium-ion, the structure of anodes and cathodes, the principles of solid-state electrolytes. That knowledge was enough to build the batteries I already had. It was not enough to leap forward a generation.

But the interface did not require me to understand everything. Only to describe the structure. If I could somehow obtain a sample of a better battery—something denser, something more advanced—I could disassemble it, map its internal architecture, and feed that map to the interface. It would build copies, even if I didn't fully grasp the quantum mechanics that made them work.

Where would I find such a battery?

The thought drifted through my mind and settled, unanswered. The alien ship, perhaps. *Whisper*. Its technology was centuries beyond anything human. Its power source might be exactly what I needed. But that was a conversation for another day, with someone I had never met, about a favor I had not yet earned.

For now, I had a boat to finish.

---

The foil mounting points were integrated into the hull structure, massive aluminium sockets that I had designed into the frame from the beginning. They were positioned exactly one-third of the way back from the bow—two of them, port and starboard, angled outward at fifteen degrees—and a third at the stern, on the centerline. The sockets were reinforced with steel collars and lined with bearing surfaces that would let the foils pivot. The hydraulic cylinders that would eventually control them were already mounted, their lines run through the hull to a central manifold in the workshop.

What I did not have were the foils themselves. Those would come later, when I had gathered more material and solved the power problem. For now, the mounting points were complete, their openings sealed with temporary covers, their hydraulics capped and pressurized with inert gas. They were ready for a future that was not yet here.

The work took all afternoon. I welded and bolted and tested, the interface helping with the precision work, my hands doing the rest. Ember emerged from the pilothouse around 3 PM, the blue teddy bear clutched in her mouth like a trophy. She dropped it at my feet on the aft deck and meowed loudly, demanding recognition of her hunting prowess.

"Very good," I said, wiping sweat from my forehead. "You've killed it."

She purred and flopped onto her side, kicking the bear with her hind legs. I watched her for a moment, this tiny, ridiculous creature who had become the center of my quiet world, and felt something loosen in my chest. The power problem was daunting. The foils were useless without a miracle. But I had a boat that moved, a workshop that functioned, and a kitten who thought I was the greatest hunter in the ocean.

It was enough. For now, it was enough.

---

By evening, the propulsion pod was fully tested and the foil mounts were sealed and secure. The *Daydream* was not complete—she might never be complete—but she was functional. She could move. She could cross oceans. She could keep me and Ember alive for years, if the fog and the monsters allowed it.

I set the autopilot on a gentle northward course, the pod humming its quiet song, and sat on the forward deck with Ember in my lap as the sun went down. The sky was a furnace of orange and rose, the sea a mirror of fading gold. The boat's new deck felt vast beneath me, a floating field of aluminium and possibility.

I thought about the flotilla, still gathering on their island. I thought about Anne, somewhere out there, watching the cycles turn. I thought about the alien ship girl and her strange, living vessel, and the power source that might one day lift my boat out of the water.

But mostly, I thought about the small blue teddy bear lying on the deck beside me, its ears already damp with kitten teeth, and the small orange cat who had fallen asleep in the crook of my arm, her purr a steady, living heartbeat against my skin.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

*Day 19. Propulsion pod integrated: 90 kW steerable electric drive, five-blade bronze propeller. Cruise 9 knots at 30 kW, max displacement speed 18+ knots at full power. Foil mounting points installed: two forward sockets, one stern, all hydraulic lines run and pressurized. Foils themselves pending solution to power requirement—estimated 3 thousand to 4 thousand kW needed for sustained flight. Current power inadequate. Long-term options: advanced battery technology (possible collaboration with aliencruiser-0001), diesel (not preferred). Deferred to later.*

*Ember has a teddy bear. She has killed it seventeen times. She is an excellent hunter. I am proud of her.*

*Tomorrow: begin superstructure finishing and interior details. The boat is a skeleton with a heart. Time to give it skin.*

I carried Ember to the master cabin and set her on the pillow. She curled around the bear, her nose tucked under its arm, and closed her eyes without a sound. I lay beside her, listening to the motor's hum, feeling the boat's gentle roll, and let the day's labor settle into a quiet, bone-deep satisfaction.

The foils would fly someday. I would find a way. But tonight, I had a ship that moved and a cat that purred, and that was more than I had ever dreamed of asking for.












### Day 20

I traveled.

It was the first truly quiet day in nearly a week—no fog, no salvage, no frantic fabrication. Just the long, clean line of the northern horizon and the steady hum of the propulsion pod pushing us through a gentle swell. The *Daydream* was a skeleton still, her decks bare aluminium and her interior walls unfinished, but she moved like a ship three times her age and twice her wisdom. The new hull slipped through the water with an ease that made the old boat feel like a bathtub toy.

Ember spent the morning in the pilothouse, the blue teddy bear clutched in her jaws. She had developed a ritual: every hour or so, she would carry the bear to a different corner of the boat, drop it, and meow loudly until I acknowledged her achievement. Then she would attack it, kill it, and drag it somewhere else. By noon, the bear had been murdered on every deck surface except the swim platform, which I had declared off-limits.

I spent the afternoon on the forward deck, legs dangling over the edge, watching the water part around the bow. The sea was a deep, untroubled blue, the sky a pale dome unmarked by clouds. I had not checked the chat in two days. I did not check it now. The world outside my hull could wait.

North. I had been heading north since the beginning, drawn by nothing more than a gut feeling and the vague promise of cooler waters. The tropics were behind me now; the air had a crisp edge to it, and the fish I caught for dinner had a different flavor—richer, oilier. Ember approved.

At sunset, I sat in the pilothouse with a cup of tea—real tea, from a small tin I'd found in the barge's deckhouse—and watched the stars come out. The navigation lights glowed green and red on the horizon. The autopilot held its course. The sea was empty, and I was alone, and for the first time in twenty days, I felt something close to peace.

I wrote in my logbook before bed:

*Day 20. Traveled north. No salvage. No emergencies. Ember killed the bear fourteen times. A good day.*
