
### Day 31

The first prototype was a failure.

I had spent the morning specifying the architecture: a 64-bit ARM-compatible processor, 8 gigabytes of RAM, 128 gigabytes of solid-state storage, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth controllers, a GPIO header, and a Linux-compatible firmware stack. The interface had accepted the design, consumed the elements I'd gathered—silicon from sand, copper from wire, gold from the ring, trace dopants from the hair and kelp—and produced a small green circuit board that looked exactly like a Raspberry Pi.

It didn't boot. The power LED lit, but the processor stayed cold. I spent two hours troubleshooting before I found the error: I had specified the wrong clock crystal frequency. The processor was waiting for a signal that never came.

I fixed the design, added more detail to the timing subsystem, and tried again. The second prototype booted but crashed after four seconds. The third prototype ran for an hour before the Wi-Fi controller overheated. The fourth prototype worked.

I sat at the workbench, staring at the tiny green board, its LEDs blinking steadily, its processor humming through a test script I'd written that morning. Ember jumped onto the bench and sniffed it. The bear dangled from her mouth.

"That," I told her, "is a fully functional single-board computer. Fabricated from sand and hair and a broken wedding ring. We are living in the future."

She touched the board with her nose, decided it was not food, and returned to her cat tree.

I fabricated three more that evening, refining the process each time. By midnight, I had a small stack of identical boards, all running Helm's core navigation code in parallel with the desktop. The AI now had a distributed neural network—slower than the GPU, but far more resilient. If one node failed, the others would continue. If the desktop died, the boat would still have a brain.

















### Day 32

I spent the day writing the software package.

The first version of Helm was not the full AI I was training on the desktop. That system was too large, too complex, too dependent on the GPU's parallel processing. The version I loaded onto the Raspberry Pi clones was a stripped-down navigation assistant: it could read sensor data, plot safe courses, avoid known fog patterns and monster territories, and maintain a steady heading in changing conditions. It could not learn. It could not make complex decisions. But it could keep a boat moving safely while the crew slept, and that was more than most survivors had.

I called it *Helm Lite*.

The package included the operating system, the navigation software, a basic manual I wrote in plain English, and a hardware bridge schematic so users could connect the board to their own boat's systems. Everything was open-source. Everything was documented. Everything was designed to be installed by someone with no programming experience and minimal tools.

Ember supervised the documentation process by sleeping on the keyboard twice.

### Day 39

The upgrade interface assembled it from braided polymer and a thin core of titanium wire—stronger than the old one, with a higher melting point and better abrasion resistance. I wound it onto the reel by hand, six hundred meters of it, and set the drag to maximum. If the fish was going to strip the spool again, it would have to break the line first.

Ember watched from the doorway, her tail flicking. She had not come onto the aft deck—the cold was too much for her—but she knew something had happened. The bear dangled from her mouth.

"Not yet," I told her. "But soon."

Fifty minutes later, the second rod screamed.

I was ready. I crossed the deck in five seconds, braced my feet, and grabbed the rod from the holder. The line was already spooling out at a terrifying rate, the drag howling, the rod tip bent nearly double. I didn't try to stop it. I let it run, counting seconds, waiting for the first sign of fatigue.

The battle lasted twenty minutes.

The fish—if it was a fish—fought with a strength that had no right to exist in something its size. It ran deep, then surfaced, then ran deep again, each burst of speed stripping meters of line despite the drag. My arms ached. My hands cramped. I braced myself against the gunwale and held on, gaining line inch by inch, losing it in sudden, violent surges.

And all the while, I thought about automation.

This was not sustainable. I was one man with two arms and a limited supply of adrenaline. If the polar waters were full of creatures like this, I needed a system that could fight them without me. A motorized reel. A variable drag controlled by machine learning. A crane that could lift the catch aboard without human intervention. I designed it in my head while the fish tried to pull me into the sea, each component crystallizing out of the struggle.

The reel motor would need to be powerful but precise—a servo with feedback control, tuned to respond to sudden acceleration without snapping the line. The drag would be algorithmic, adjusting in real time based on the fish's behavior. The crane could be the same one I already had, just programmed to swing out and retrieve once the fish was at the surface.

I filed the plans away as the fish finally began to tire.

When I got it to the surface—three meters down, still fighting—I saw the glow.

It was bioluminescent. Not the faint, scattered shimmer of deep-sea plankton, but a geometric, pulsing light that traced patterns across the creature's flanks. The patterns shifted as I watched, spirals and chevrons of pale blue-green that seemed almost deliberate, like a language written in photons. The fish was as long as my arm and thin as my wrist, its body streamlined to an almost impossible degree. Its tail was a rigid, paddle-like structure that vibrated even now, a blur of motion my eyes couldn't quite track.

I almost let the line go. The beauty of it—the sheer, alien beauty—hit me in a way I wasn't prepared for. This was not a monster. This was not a threat. This was a living thing that had evolved in the crushing dark of the polar deep, and it was magnificent.

But I needed it.

I hauled it aboard with the crane, my hands shaking from exhaustion and something close to awe. The fish flopped on the deck, its geometric patterns pulsing faster now, its paddle-tail hammering against the aluminium with a sound like a rapid drumroll. I knelt beside it, studying the cellular structure I could just barely make out through its translucent skin—layers of tissue that seemed to store and conduct energy in ways that went far beyond anything I understood.

I photographed it. I documented everything I could see. And then I contacted Anne.

### Day 40

The automatic fishing reel was ugly and functional and probably the best piece of engineering I'd done since the hydrofoils.

I fabricated it in a single morning, fueled by coffee and the lingering frustration of yesterday's deal. The motor was a servo unit I'd salvaged from the barge weeks ago, its bearings replaced with stainless steel, its controller linked to Helm's sensor array. The reel was aluminium, oversized, capable of holding a thousand meters of the reinforced line. The drag was fully algorithmic—Helm monitored the line tension, the spool speed, and the angle of the rod, adjusting the resistance in real time to fight the fish without breaking the line.

The whole assembly mounted on a reinforced bracket beside the original rod holder, its power cable running to the boat's main bus. A second unit, identical, replaced the other rod holder. I had two automated fishing stations now, each capable of doing what I'd done yesterday, without my arms ever getting tired.

The crane, I reprogrammed to respond to a signal from Helm. When a fish was at the surface, the crane would swing out, lower a net, and lift the catch aboard. The only thing I had to do was set the bait and press a button.

Ember watched the fabrication process from the heater vent, her eyes tracking the shimmering cloud of particles with the detached interest of a creature who had seen too many miracles to be impressed by one more. The bear was under her paw. The bear was always under her paw.

I tested the system at noon. The bait went in, the line went out, and the reel hummed quietly, waiting. I went inside and worked on Helm's code while the machines did their work.
