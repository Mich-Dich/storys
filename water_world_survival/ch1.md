### Day 41

I had gone too far.

The realization came slowly, creeping in like the cold through a poorly sealed hatch. The sea had changed again—darker now, heavier, the swells longer and deeper. The sky was a pale, bruised purple at the horizon, and the air smelled of something ancient and metallic. The sonar showed the seabed dropping away into depths that made the abyssal plain look shallow. The temperature had fallen another two degrees overnight.

I stood at the pilothouse windows with a mug of tea, watching the world grow stranger, and admitted to myself that Anne had been right. The polar waters were not for beginners. I had pushed north with the confidence of a man who had survived thirty-four days and built a boat that could cross oceans. But survival and readiness were not the same thing.

I set a new course: south. Not a retreat—a correction. I would backtrack to the latitude where I'd caught the glow-eel, where the fishing was still possible and the cold was still manageable, and I would wait there until I had what I needed. The *Daydream*'s bow swung around, the propulsion pod humming, and the grey horizon shifted from fore to aft.

I had been traveling for perhaps an hour when I saw the whale die.

The spout first—a distant plume of white against the grey, maybe eight kilometers off the starboard bow. I lifted my binoculars, expecting to see the slow, majestic rise of a leviathan's back. What I saw instead made me set the tea down and kill the throttle to an idle.

The whale was a true whale—not a Glass Sail Leviathan, but something closer to a fin whale, sixty feet long, its dark back slick with water. It was breaching, but not in the graceful arc of a creature at play. It was thrashing. Violently. Desperately. The water around it was boiling.

At first I couldn't see why. Then one of the smaller shapes jumped, and I understood.

The fish were small—no longer than my forearm—but they were built like hatchets. Their bodies were silver and streamlined, and on their heads they carried a blade: a flat, axe-like protrusion of bone that glinted in the pale light. They moved in a school of thousands, a living cloud of blades that swarmed the whale from every direction. They grazed its flanks, leaving long, shallow cuts that bloomed red in the grey water. They struck from below, launching themselves out of the sea like missiles, and then they dove and struck again.

And some of them jumped higher—much higher—and came down head-first, their axe-blades driving into the whale's back with a force that must have been devastating. I watched one arc through the air, a silver needle against the grey sky, and slam into the whale just behind the blowhole. The whale convulsed. A fountain of blood sprayed across the surface.

The school was relentless. They didn't bite. They didn't tear. They cut. Hundreds of cuts, thousands, each one shallow but collectively fatal. The whale was bleeding from a hundred wounds, its thrashing growing weaker, its spout ragged and pink. The water around it had turned a deep, arterial red.

I watched for five minutes. Ten. The whale stopped thrashing. The school continued to circle, continued to cut, but the frenzy was slowing. The body began to sink, and the fish followed it down, a silver tornado spiraling into the dark.

I did not accelerate. I did not start the motor. I turned the wheel very, very gently and let the *Daydream* drift away from the kill site, the propulsion pod barely turning over, the noise signature as low as I could make it. I had no idea if the axe-head fish were attracted to sound or motion or electrical fields. I had no intention of finding out.

Ember was at the window, her ears flat, her tail bushed to twice its normal size. She had watched the whole thing. The bear was on the floor, forgotten.

"That," I said quietly, "is why we're going south."

She didn't respond. She didn't need to.

I traveled at low speed for the rest of the day, the radar sweeping constantly, the sonar pinging for any sign of approaching schools. Nothing followed. The sea returned to its grey, empty calm. By evening, I had put fifty kilometers between myself and the kill site, and the water temperature had crept back up to merely cold.

Before bed, I added a new entry to my growing catalog of Expanse fauna:

*Specimen 002 — Provisional Name: Axe-Head Razorfish (Securis ichthys)*

*Length: 30-40 cm. Body plan: streamlined, silver, with a prominent axe-like bony blade on the dorsal cranium. Schooling predator. Observed hunting a 18-meter baleen whale via sustained superficial cutting and high-velocity diving strikes. The blade is used to inflict hundreds of shallow lacerations, resulting in exsanguination. Method suggests high coordination and possibly communication within the school. Speed: unknown, but jumping behavior implies significant burst acceleration. Threat level: extreme to any warm-blooded creature in the water. Do not approach. Do not attract attention. Avoid entirely.*

*Note: The whale was killed in under fifteen minutes. I have seen monsters up close. This was worse. The monsters at least feel like something from a nightmare. The razorfish feel like something evolution built for a purpose I don't want to understand.*


















### Day 42

I fished.

All day, from the pale grey dawn to the long polar twilight, I fished. The automated reels hummed their quiet song, the lines spooled out into the deep, and the crane swung fish after fish onto the deck. Cod. Halibut. Something with too many eyes that I threw back. A small shark that Ember hissed at from the safety of the pilothouse.

But no glow-eel. No bioluminescent patterns. No explosive, reel-stripping speed. The deep water where I'd caught the first one was empty of miracles.

By afternoon, I had accumulated nearly three hundred pounds of fish. The freezers were full. The aft deck was stacked with insulated crates. I could have fed a small fleet for a week. Instead, I contacted the *Nimitz*.

The trade negotiation was brief and, from my perspective, mildly insulting.

**daydream-0001** said: "I have 280 pounds of fresh polar fish—cod, halibut, and something that tastes like sea bass. I want beef. Real beef. The kind that comes from a cow."

**Nimitz-Provisioner** responded: "We have beef. It's rare. The herd is small and the feed is limited. Exchange rate is 70 to 1. 70 pounds of fish for 1 pound of beef."

**daydream-0001** said: "Seventy to one? That's extortion."

**Nimitz-Provisioner** said: "That's economics. Fish is abundant in the north. Beef is not. Do you want the steak or not?"

**daydream-0001** said: "I want the steak. Sending 280 pounds of fish."

The trade materialized on the galley counter: four vacuum-sealed packages, each containing one pound of dark red, perfectly marbled beef. Premium steak. The kind of meat that had cost a fortune in the old world and was literally irreplaceable in the new one. I held one of the packages up to the light and felt something that was not quite hunger and not quite nostalgia.

Ember sniffed the package and meowed. She knew what beef was. She had never tasted it—she had been a tiny kitten when I found her—but she knew.

"You can have a small piece," I told her. "A very small piece. This cost me 70 pounds of fish per pound."

She meowed again, more insistently.

That evening, I cooked.

The galley was cold, but I had prepared for this. I lit the portable induction hob I'd moved to the pilothouse—behind the insulated curtain, where the heater kept the air warm—and set a cast-iron pan on its surface. The steak went in with a pat of butter I'd fabricated from fish oil and salt, and the smell that filled the pilothouse was so rich, so primal, so utterly out of place in this world of salt and cold and death that I had to sit down for a moment.

Ember sat on the cat tree, the bear under her paw, her eyes fixed on the pan. Her nose twitched with every sizzle.

I seared the steak to medium-rare—I had a thermometer, salvaged from a wreck—and let it rest while I prepared the rest of the meal. Rehydrated potatoes from the *Nimitz*'s stores. A handful of dried herbs, crushed between my fingers. A small glass of water that I pretended was wine.

I ate at the nav desk, the desktop glowing beside me, Helm's neural network pulsing in slow, steady rhythms. The steak was perfect. Charred on the outside, pink and tender within, the fat rendered to a buttery softness that melted on my tongue. I closed my eyes and for a moment—just a moment—I was not on a boat in an endless ocean, fighting for survival against monsters and fog and the slow attrition of time. I was a man eating a good dinner, and that was enough.

Ember got a small piece, cut into tiny cubes. She ate them with the slow, deliberate pleasure of a creature who understood that this was a rare and precious thing. Then she licked her whiskers, jumped onto my lap, and purred.

"Good, right?" I said.

She closed her eyes. The bear was on the floor, forgotten.

I sat in the warm pilothouse, the empty plate on the desk, the cat on my lap, the sea dark and cold beyond the windows. Tomorrow I would fish again. Tomorrow I would keep searching for the glow-eel that held the key to everything I wanted to build. But tonight, I had a full stomach and a warm boat and a small orange creature who thought I was the best hunter in the world.

It was enough.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

*Day 42. 643 souls. Fished all day in the glow-eel's previous location. No second specimen. Freezer capacity exceeded; traded 280 lbs of fish to Nimitz at 70:1 exchange for 4 lbs of premium beef. Cooked one steak. Ember approved. Worth every ounce of fish.*

*The northern waters hold wonders and terrors in equal measure. The axe-head razorfish are a reminder that the Expanse has many ways to kill, and not all of them are monsters. I am proceeding south to more familiar latitudes while I continue the search.*

*Tomorrow: continue fishing. The solution is out there. I just have to be patient.*















### Day 43

The fish were not cooperating.

I spent the day working the automated reels in the same stretch of water where I'd landed the first glow-eel, the *Daydream* tracing slow circles over the deep. The sonar showed scattered schools at depth, the occasional large signature that might have been a curious seal or a small whale. The reels hummed, the lines spooled out and back, and the crane deposited a steady stream of cod, halibut, and something silver and oily that Ember refused to eat. By mid-afternoon, the freezers were full again and I was running out of crates.

But no geometric patterns. No bioluminescence. No explosive, reel-screaming strikes. Just ordinary fish, as if the ocean had decided to hoard its miracles.

I was at the nav desk, trading another batch of halibut to the *Nimitz* for a crate of capacitors, when the chat began to stir with something more interesting than fish.

---

**battleship-0023** said: "We've completed a full survey of the southern spawn region. The water here is temperate—not tropical, not polar. Warm summers, cool winters. It's a huge band of ocean, maybe 2,000 kilometers across, bounded by colder water to the north and warmer water to the south. We've mapped the monster territories within it. They're predictable. The four-day timer works. The month-long timer seems to exist, but the territories are large enough that a moving vessel can stay within them for weeks without triggering anything. It's... manageable."

**speedboat-5601** said: "We went further south, past the boundary. The water got warmer fast. Tropical islands, coral reefs, palm trees. Beautiful. But the monsters are different down there. Bigger. Weirder. We turned back after two days. It felt like we'd walked into someone else's territory."

**battleship-0023** said: "So the spawn region is a middle ground. Not too hot, not too cold. The threats here are serious but survivable if you follow the rules. Outside this band, north or south, the difficulty increases. We think the system dropped us here for a reason. It's a training zone."

**sailboat-1190** said: "That makes a horrible kind of sense. The first month teaches you the basics. After that, you're supposed to venture out. But if you go too far too fast..."

**battleship-0023** said: "Exactly. Daydream, you're the furthest north of anyone in our cycle. What are you seeing?"

---

I set down my coffee and composed a response. The pieces had been falling into place since Day 41, since the whale and the silver blades and the water turning red. The flotilla's discovery confirmed what I had already begun to suspect.

**daydream-0001** said: "The spawn region is the starter zone. The Expanse put us there to learn the basics: movement, crafting, trade, territory timers. It's the safest water we'll ever have. The further you go from it, the harder the game gets."

**daydream-0001** said: "I'm currently at 68° north. Three days ago I watched a school of fish—small ones, 30 centimeters—kill a 20-meter whale in under fifteen minutes. They had axe-blades on their heads. They cut it to pieces while it was still alive. That's what's waiting outside the starter zone. Not just bigger monsters. New kinds of threats. Things that don't follow the rules you've learned."

**daydream-0001** said: "I'm not saying don't explore. Exploration is how we find resources, map territories, build things. But don't go alone. Don't go unprepared. The flotilla model is the right one. Groups of ships, moving together, sharing watches and resources. If I'd encountered those razorfish in a smaller boat—if I'd been anchoring instead of drifting—I might not be sending this message."

**daydream-0001** said: "The spawn region is a gift. Use it to prepare. When you leave it, leave it carefully."

**fishcutter-0491** responded: "Razorfish? Axe-blades? That's... new. And terrifying."

**battleship-0023** said: "We'll incorporate this into the fleet's navigation brief. Daydream, thank you. Again."

**ketch-0817** said: "The Helm database needs a threat classification update. I'll add the razorfish and the polar leviathans based on your reports. If anyone else has documented new species, send the data."

**aliencruiser-0001** said: "The ship is showing me something. A map of the world, with rings. Concentric circles. The spawn region is the innermost ring. Outside that, things get... stranger. I don't think the Expanse is a flat difficulty curve. I think it's a spiral. The further you go, the deeper you get."

**daydream-0001** said: "That matches what I've seen. Stay in formation. Share what you learn. And don't swim in unfamiliar water."

---

I closed the chat and sat back, Ember a warm weight on my lap. The bear was under her chin, its single remaining ear damp with kitten teeth. Outside, the grey sea stretched to the horizon, empty and patient. The reels hummed. The crane swung another halibut onto the deck.

The spawn region was a training zone. The Expanse had given us a month to learn the rules before throwing us into deeper water. Some of us—the island people—had refused to learn. Some had learned the wrong lessons. But the ones who were still alive, still moving, still sharing knowledge—they had a chance.

I just had to make sure I didn't push too far, too fast. Anne had warned me. The razorfish had warned me. The cold and the dark and the long polar twilight had warned me. I was listening.




















### Day 44

The first glow-eel hit at 09:42.

I was in the workshop, fabricating a spare gear for the crane's winch, when the automated reel's alarm shrieked through the boat. Not the steady beep of a normal strike—the continuous, climbing scream of line spooling out at maximum speed. I dropped the gear and ran.

By the time I reached the aft deck, the reel was already fighting. The algorithmic drag had engaged, the servo motor whining as it adjusted resistance in real time. The line was spooling out in controlled bursts, the rod tip bent into a deep, shuddering curve. Helm's interface on the deck display showed the telemetry: line tension 80%, spool speed decreasing, the fish beginning to tire.

I didn't have to touch it. I stood in the cold wind, watching my machine do what my arms had done four days ago, and felt a strange, proud affection for the collection of aluminium and code that was fighting my battle.

The fight lasted twelve minutes—faster than my manual attempt, the algorithmic drag wearing the fish down with an efficiency no human wrist could match. When the glow-eel broke the surface, its geometric patterns pulsing with that alien blue-green light, I was ready with the crane. The net swung out, scooped it from the water, and deposited it gently on the deck.

It was beautiful. As long as my arm and thin as my wrist, its translucent skin shimmering with spirals and chevrons of bioluminescence. The rigid paddle-tail hammered against the aluminium deck, a blur of motion my eyes couldn't track. The light patterns pulsed faster, slower, faster—a language I couldn't read.

"Got you," I said, kneeling beside it. "Finally. I got you."

Ember watched from the pilothouse doorway, her tail bushed. She did not approve of the glow-eel. It was too strange, too bright, too unlike anything a cat should have to deal with. She retreated to her cat tree, the bear clutched in her mouth, and glared at me through the window.

I was too elated to apologize.

I was carrying the eel to the workshop—carefully, in a tub of seawater, trying to keep it alive long enough to extract the cellular template—when the second alarm went off.

I froze. The tub sloshed. The first eel pulsed and hammered its tail.

The second reel was screaming.

"No," I said. "No, that's not possible."

I set the tub down and ran to the aft deck. The second rod was bent double, the line spooling out, the servo motor whining under the load. The display showed another strike—same profile, same explosive acceleration, same deep-water origin. Two glow-eels in under fifteen minutes, after days of nothing.

I stood between the two stations, one eel already caught and the second fighting the machine, and I started to laugh. It was not a sane laugh. It was the laugh of a man who had fished for a week in the freezing polar sea, who had traded away his first miracle for a set of plans, who had been beginning to wonder if he would ever find another. It was the laugh of a man whose luck had finally, impossibly, turned.

The second eel surfaced eight minutes later. The crane swung it aboard. I stood on the aft deck with two glowing fish, two biological supercapacitors, two keys to the future I'd been building since the day I first sketched hydrofoils on a scrap of plywood.

I carried them both to the workshop and got to work.

---

Anne's plans were a masterpiece of reverse-engineering. The cellular architecture of the glow-eel's capacitor tissue was unlike anything human science had ever built—layers of lipid membrane folded into fractal patterns, interspersed with proteins that functioned as molecular-scale charge carriers. The eel stored energy not in chemical bonds, like a lithium battery, but in the physical tension of charged membranes, a mechanism that was closer to a muscle than a battery. The energy density was staggering. The discharge rate was limited only by the melting point of the conductors.

The interface could not design something like this from scratch. But it could copy. I fed it the cellular template—a three-dimensional map of the eel's energy-storage tissue, extracted through a process Anne had documented in exhaustive detail—and the raw organic precursors from the first specimen. The cloud rose, shimmering and golden-green, and when it settled, a single battery cell sat on the workbench.

It was the size of a car battery, but it weighed less than a third as much. Its casing was a translucent composite, through which I could see the faint, pulsing glow of the biological material within. The terminals were gold-plated copper, the connectors standardized to match the *Daydream*'s existing bus. I hooked up a voltmeter.

The numbers made me sit down.

One cell stored as much energy as my entire 40-kilowatt-hour lithium bank—the bank I'd spent weeks building, trading, salvaging, expanding. And its discharge rate was ten times higher. I could pull 400 kilowatts from this single unit without exceeding its rated capacity. Two cells, in parallel, could feed a 3,500-kilowatt motor for the brief, glorious bursts that would lift the *Daydream* out of the water and let her fly.

I fabricated a second cell from the second eel. It took twenty minutes. When it was done, I carried both cells to the power room and connected them to the main bus. The old lithium bank—forty kilowatt-hours of salvaged cells, copper bus bars, and careful engineering—sat beside them, suddenly obsolete.

I disconnected it. The boat's systems didn't even flicker. The biological cells handled the entire load without a twitch.

I sat on the floor of the power room, staring at the two pulsing, glowing boxes that had just replaced the heart of my boat, and felt something I hadn't felt since the day I'd woken on this ocean. Not satisfaction. Not pride. Completion. The power problem was solved. The foils could fly.

Ember poked her head through the doorway, the bear in her mouth. She sniffed the air, decided the new batteries were not interesting, and meowed for dinner.

"In a minute," I said. "I need to make a trade first."

---

The old lithium bank was still a fortune in stored energy. I posted the offer with a price that reflected its value and my needs.

**daydream-0001** said: "Trading: complete 40 kWh lithium battery bank with integrated charge controllers and bus bars. Fully functional. Just removed from my vessel. Asking: steel plate, 6mm minimum thickness, marine-grade or better. Enough to reinforce a 42-meter hull bottom from bow to stern. Also accepting: titanium plate, ceramic composite armor, or equivalent hull reinforcement materials."

**battleship-0023** responded: "We have steel. A lot of it. The *Iowa*'s original armor belt was partially removed decades ago, but the spares are still in the hold. 40 kWh of lithium would let us run our auxiliary systems without burning diesel. How much steel do you need?"

**daydream-0001** said: "Enough to cover the underside of a 42-meter hull. Overlap plates, welded seams. I want to be able to shrug off anything that tries to come through the bottom."

**battleship-0023** said: "We can do that. Sending the steel. Fair trade?"

**daydream-0001** said: "Fair trade."

The lithium bank dissolved into the interface's light, and the steel appeared on the foredeck: heavy, dark grey plates, military-grade, each one stamped with a lot number from a foundry that had probably been gone for decades. I would install them tomorrow, a new skin for the *Daydream*'s belly, armor against the razorfish and whatever else the deep might send.

But tonight, I sat in the pilothouse with Ember on my lap, the two biological batteries humming in the power room, the hydrofoils folded against the hull, and the future stretched out before me like a sea I had finally learned to sail.

Before bed, I wrote in my logbook:

*Day 44. 643 souls. Caught two glow-eels within 15 minutes using automated fishing stations. Fabricated two biological capacitor cells from Anne's schematics. Each cell matches the capacity of my entire previous lithium bank; discharge rate is an order of magnitude higher. Old bank traded to the battleship for hull armor steel. The power problem is solved.*

*The foils can fly. I need to design and fabricate the new motor—something capable of the 3,500 kW peak the foils require—but the energy storage is no longer a constraint. The* Daydream *will lift out of the water. It's only a matter of time.*

*Ember remains unimpressed by the revolution in energy storage. She is more interested in her dinner. I am more interested in what comes next.*
























### Day 45

The steel went onto the hull like armor onto a warship.

I woke early, fed Ember, and spent the morning preparing the *Daydream* for the reinforcement. The plates from the battleship were heavy—far heavier than anything I'd worked with before—and the crane groaned as I positioned them on the foredeck. Each plate was two meters by three, dark grey, stamped with the faded insignia of a navy that no longer existed. They had been forged to stop shells and torpedoes. They would stop razorfish.

The upgrade interface made the installation seamless. I designed the reinforcement as a single integrated layer: the steel plates bonded to the *Daydream*'s existing hull with a composite adhesive, the seams welded and sealed, the leading edges tapered to reduce drag. The bow received an extra layer, a sharp, reinforced prow that would cut through anything short of a leviathan. The stern, where the propulsion pod and the rear foil mounted, got double plating and a set of sacrificial zinc anodes I fabricated from scrap.

The cloud rose, silver and grey and heavy, and when it settled, the *Daydream* sat a fraction lower in the water, her belly armored, her skin thick with borrowed warship steel. I ran my hand along the new plating at the waterline. It was cold and smooth and utterly unyielding.

Ember watched from the pilothouse window, the bear in her mouth. She had grown accustomed to the boat transforming around her. It no longer alarmed her. It was simply what the world did.

I spent the afternoon testing the new hull in the cold grey swells. The *Daydream* handled the same as before—the added weight was minimal, the drag increase barely noticeable at cruising speed. But I felt different at the helm. Safer. The image of the whale, bleeding from a hundred cuts, had stayed with me. Now, if a school of razorfish found the *Daydream*, they would find nothing but steel.

















### Day 46

The motor was the largest fabrication I had ever attempted.

I had been gathering materials for weeks without knowing it. The trades—copper from the fishcutter, rare-earth magnets from Anne, electronic scrap from a dozen different survivors, steel and aluminium and titanium from the barge and the battleship and the *Nimitz*—had all been building toward this. I had a stockpile that would have made an engineer weep, and I was going to use every gram of it.

The design took most of the morning. The motor I needed was not a marine unit. It was an aircraft engine in all but name—a compact, liquid-cooled, permanent-magnet synchronous motor capable of producing 5,000 kilowatts at peak output. The continuous rating would be lower, perhaps 3,500 kilowatts, but that was all the foils needed. The motor would drive a shaft that ran down through the rear hydrofoil strut, ending in a contra-rotating propeller set that would push the *Daydream* out of the water and keep her there.

The old propulsion pod—the 90-kilowatt unit I had been so proud of two weeks ago—would be retired. The new motor was too large to fit in a steerable pod. It would be mounted inside the hull, in a reinforced compartment above the rear foil mounting point, with the drive shaft running down through the foil strut itself. This meant the rear foil could never fully retract—the shaft and propeller needed to stay clear of the hull—but it could extend further when the boat was foilborne, dropping the propeller deeper into the water for maximum thrust.

The design was radical. It was also the only way to make the geometry work.

I opened the upgrade interface and began to specify. The stator windings. The rotor geometry. The cooling channels. The bearing surfaces. The shaft coupling. Every detail I could imagine, I fed to the interface, and the interface responded by sharpening the design, optimizing the material usage, reducing the cost. The cloud that rose from my stockpile was the largest I had ever seen—a swirling nebula of copper and steel and rare-earth elements that blotted out the grey polar sky.

When it settled, the motor sat on the workshop floor. It was the size of a compact car and dense as a collapsed star. The shaft coupling was a precision-ground titanium fitting that would connect to the drive shaft I would fabricate tomorrow. The cooling lines were pre-plumbed, the electrical connections standardized. It was, without question, the most powerful electric motor ever mounted on a private vessel. It was probably the most powerful electric motor in the history of the Expanse.

I named it *Heart*.

Installing it took the rest of the day. The old propulsion pod came out with the crane, its mounting well sealed and reinforced. The new motor went into the compartment I had designed for it, bolted to the hull frame, its shaft housing extending down through the rear foil strut. The drive shaft itself—a two-meter length of titanium alloy, fabricated from the last of my rare-metal stock—slid into place with micrometer precision. The contra-rotating propellers, twin three-blade bronze units spinning in opposite directions, mounted on the shaft's end.

By evening, the *Daydream* had a new heart. The old pod sat on the foredeck, suddenly a relic, waiting to be traded or recycled. The new motor hummed when I ran the diagnostic, a deep, smooth vibration that resonated through the hull like a second pulse.

I didn't test it. Not yet. There was one more piece to build.

---

The cockpit was the hardest design I had ever attempted.

The problem was dimensionality. A boat operated in two dimensions—heading and speed, left and right, forward and back. A hydrofoil operated in three. When the *Daydream* lifted out of the water, she would need to be controlled like an aircraft: pitch, roll, and yaw, in addition to throttle. The helm station I had been using for forty-six days was not equipped for that. I needed something new.

I spent hours at the desktop, running simulations, sketching layouts, discarding designs. The cockpit needed to be intuitive—I would be flying the boat alone, with no copilot, and I couldn't afford to fumble with unfamiliar controls in an emergency. It needed to integrate with Helm, which would handle the fine adjustments while I focused on the broad movements. It needed to be comfortable enough for long flights but secure enough for violent maneuvers.

And it needed to be placed correctly. The pilot of a flying boat needs to feel the motion—needs to be at the center of pitch and roll, where the forces are most directly perceived. For the *Daydream*, that point was directly above the forward hydrofoils, about a third of the way back from the bow.

The pilothouse was in the wrong place. It sat amidships, optimized for visibility but not for flight feel. I couldn't rebuild the entire superstructure. But I could extend it.

The design crystallized in the late afternoon: a protruding cockpit module, like a bubble canopy on a fighter jet, extending forward from the pilothouse's upper deck. It would be positioned directly above the forward foil mounts, giving me the sensory feedback I needed. The controls would be a hybrid—a center stick for pitch and roll, rudder pedals for yaw, a throttle quadrant on the left. The displays would be configured like a glass cockpit: primary flight display in the center, navigation on the right, systems status on the left. Helm's interface would be integrated into every screen.

The fabrication was complex but fast. The interface consumed the last of my aluminium stock, a crate of electronic components from the *Nimitz*, and a sheet of armored glass I'd been saving for weeks. The cloud rose, silver and blue and transparent, and when it settled, the cockpit was there.

I climbed the new ladder—three steps up from the pilothouse—and stood inside it for the first time. The canopy wrapped around me, a dome of polarized glass that gave a nearly 360-degree view. The stick was where my right hand expected it. The throttles were under my left. The screens glowed with Helm's steady, patient presence, waiting for input.

I sat in the pilot's seat—a carbon-fiber shell with five-point harnesses—and gripped the stick. The boat was still in the water, the foils retracted, the new motor idling at diagnostic levels. But for a moment, I could feel it. The lift. The speed. The flight.

Ember climbed the ladder behind me—a new skill, one she had developed out of stubbornness—and jumped onto a small shelf I had included for her. It was padded, with a lip to keep her from sliding, and positioned so she could see out the canopy. The bear was in her mouth. She surveyed the cockpit with the critical eye of a ship's cat who had seen many upgrades and was not easily impressed.

"Good enough?" I asked her.

She curled into the padded shelf, tucked the bear under her chin, and closed her eyes. Approval.

I set a new course before I climbed down to the master cabin. South. Toward the *Nimitz*, toward warmer water, toward a stretch of sea where I could test the foils without freezing to death if something went wrong. If the motor failed. If the hull breached. If the thousand things that could go wrong with an untested flying boat decided to go wrong all at once.

Tomorrow, I would test. Tomorrow, the *Daydream* would fly, or she would fail, and I would learn from the failure and try again.

The island was volcanic, young and restless beneath its moss-covered skin.

The heat pump was a simple device in principle: a closed loop of pipes running through the hot spring's source, a heat exchanger to transfer the warmth to a secondary loop of freshwater, and a network of radiators throughout the boat. Quality of life. It was not a luxury. It was the difference between enduring the Expanse and living in it.
